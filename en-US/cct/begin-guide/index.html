The Guide For Beginner

Coordinates Calculation Theory#

MCBE Coordinate Arithmetic, from Basics to Advanced

About This Book#

Coordinates Calculation Theory (CCT) is what the MCBE command community calls a family of geometric coordinate techniques. It uses the virtual positions and rotations built up by execute to make the game itself act as a calculator, carrying out geometric constructions — midpoints, mirrors, rotations, scaling — in a pure-command environment, and from there drawing circles, ellipses, cube frames, and all kinds of symmetric figures.

This book starts with the coordinate syntax of execute, explains why CCT works, then begins from the simplest case — the midpoint — and walks step by step to far-point reflection, double-reflection rotation, and figure composition. It closes with a unified algebraic description and a formula reference.

Everything before Chapter 5 needs only junior-high math: similar triangles, the Pythagorean theorem, and the difference-of-squares identity. Chapter 6 brings in vectors and matrices.

Chapter 2　How CCT Works#

The last chapter covered how to "walk a distance along the rotation." This chapter explains why that action, paired with a very large distance, computes a ratio point.

The only math involved is similar triangles and the Pythagorean theorem.

2.1 facing gives you a direction#

First, be clear about what facing actually does.

Let the current position be P and the target be Q. After facing, the line of sight points at Q; now walk d blocks:

You arrive at the point starting from P, heading toward Q, at a distance of exactly d.

The key is that the length is exactly d. No matter how far apart P and Q are, the direction facing produces always has length 1. It compresses "the displacement from P to Q" down to unit length.

This means one thing: facing hands you a direction for free, and hidden in that direction is ratio information about the relative positions of P and Q. CCT is about extracting that ratio.

The extraction method is to construct a right triangle and then use similar triangles.

2.2 Finding the midpoint with similar triangles#

Let S and M be two points at the same height, separated by a horizontal distance D. We want their midpoint.

Step one: raise the origin far above S, to a height denoted H. Call this new position S′.

Step two: look from S′ toward M. That line of sight is the hypotenuse of right triangle S′SM, so by Pythagoras:

L = \sqrt{H^2 + D^2}

Step three: walk H/2 blocks along that hypotenuse.

The path travelled has the same direction as the whole hypotenuse, so the small triangle it forms is similar to S′SM, with ratio:

\frac{H/2}{L}

So the actual horizontal movement is:

\frac{H/2}{L}\times D = \frac{HD}{2L}

And the actual vertical drop is:

\frac{H/2}{L}\times H = \frac{H^2}{2L}

Step four: the height hasn't returned to the starting level yet, so drop the rest with relative coordinates.

Now the crucial step. When H is much larger than D:

L = \sqrt{H^2+D^2} = H\sqrt{1+\frac{D^2}{H^2}} \approx H

because D^2/H^2 is a tiny number. Substituting back into the horizontal movement:

\frac{HD}{2L} \approx \frac{HD}{2H} = \frac{D}{2}

Exactly half the horizontal distance. The midpoint is found.

Meanwhile the vertical drop is about H/2, so walking down another H/2 in step four returns you to the starting height.

Generalizing to an arbitrary ratio

Replace H/2 with tH, and the horizontal movement becomes:

\frac{tHD}{L} \approx tD

The vertical drop is about tH, taking you from height H down to about (1-t)H, so step four must drop another (1-t)H.

That's the whole content of the ratio-point formula.

2.3 The compass analogy and the role of the large number#

Look back at the process: we used a fixed length H as a "radius," and located a new point by turning a direction and cutting off a length. This matches the logic of compass construction — bisecting a segment or dropping a perpendicular with straightedge and compass also comes down to cutting intersections with circles of fixed radius.

The difference is that a traditional compass has a radius comparable to the scale of the figure, whereas here H must be far larger than the figure's scale. The approximation L \approx H only holds under that condition. That large number is the whole engine of the technique, and it's why "large" matters more than "big enough."

2.4 Where the error comes from#

The last section made an approximation: treating \sqrt{H^2+D^2} as H. That approximation isn't exact, so neither is the result.

Let's quantify. The exact horizontal movement is:

\frac{tHD}{\sqrt{H^2+D^2}}

The ideal value is tD. Expanding the radical:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{H^2+D^2}} = \frac{1}{H}\left(1+\frac{D^2}{H^2}\right)^{-1/2} \approx \frac{1}{H}\left(1-\frac{D^2}{2H^2}\right)

So:

\frac{tHD}{\sqrt{H^2+D^2}} \approx tD - \frac{tD^3}{2H^2}

Horizontal error:

E_x \approx \frac{tD^3}{2H^2}

The vertical case is analogous. Walking tH along the hypotenuse drops tH^2/L, and we compensate with tH, so the discrepancy is:

E_y \approx \frac{tD^2}{2H}

Two conclusions to remember

First, error grows with D, and the horizontal error grows as the cube of D.

Second, the horizontal error is inversely proportional to H^2, while the vertical error is only inversely proportional to H. The vertical error decays more slowly, so it becomes noticeable first.

Plugging in numbers (t = 0.5, H = 100000):

2.5 How big should H be#

From the formula, bigger H is better — but in practice you can't increase it without limit.

Game coordinates are stored as finite-precision floating point. The defining property of floating point is fixed relative precision: the larger the value, the larger the smallest representable interval. When H reaches the hundred-thousand range while the figure we care about is only a few blocks across, the low-order bits of the small coordinates get swallowed by the large one during computation.

As a model, total error is the sum of two terms:

E(H) \approx C_1\frac{r^2}{H} + C_2\varepsilon H

The first term is geometric truncation error, which shrinks as H grows. The second is floating-point error, which grows as H grows. r is the typical scale of the figure and ε is floating-point relative precision.

Total error is minimized when the two terms are equal:

H \sim \frac{r}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}

Estimating with single precision, \varepsilon \approx 2^{-23}, gives 1/\sqrt{\varepsilon} \approx 2900, that is:

H \sim 2900\,r

This explains why community formulas so often use 1024, 2048, 4096, 16384 — they land roughly in the right band for figures a few to a few dozen blocks across.

This estimate gives only an order of magnitude. The real practice is to sweep several values of H and compare:

When changing H, note that every related distance in the formula must change with it. The midpoint formula uses H, tH, and (1-t)H in three places; changing one without the others breaks it outright. Far-point reflection in Chapter 4 uses H, 2H, H in three places — same story.

2.6 Summary#

Everything CCT rests on is one approximation:

\sqrt{H^2+D^2} \approx H \qquad (H \gg D)

From it follow two facts you must keep in mind:

  • CCT is an asymptotic approximation, not an exact identity
  • There's an optimal band for H; bigger is not always better

Understanding this matters. The error analysis of every advanced formula later on is a variation and extension of this one point. When a figure shows slight deviation, fuzziness, or doubled lines, your first thought should be "is the relationship between H and the figure's scale appropriate?"

An experiment to build intuition

Place two armor stands, named S and M, at the same height, about 20 blocks apart. Use the midpoint command from 3.1 to spawn a particle, and set H to 1000, 10000, and 100000 in turn. At this range the three settings are nearly indistinguishable — which is precisely the point: precision at short range is extremely high.

Then move the two armor stands 500+ blocks apart and repeat. This time H = 1000 shows obvious deviation, and only H = 100000 gets back near the midpoint. That's E_y \approx tD^2/2H at work.

3.3 Separating yaw from pitch#

facing has one characteristic: it changes yaw and pitch together.

That's fine when S and M are at the same height. But if the two points differ in height, the direction facing produces is slanted, and positioned ^ ^ ^d changes both horizontal position and height. Sometimes we only want one of those components.

The fix is to override the unwanted angle with rotated after facing.

Keep only the horizontal direction

rotated ~ 0 means "keep yaw, set pitch to 0." After it the line of sight is horizontal, retaining only the horizontal heading toward the target. Now ^ ^ ^5 moves purely within the horizontal plane.

Keep only the vertical direction

rotated 0 ~ forces yaw to 0, keeping only pitch.

Why this trick matters

facing gives you a three-dimensional direction with all three components mixed together. Adding one rotated strips away part of that information, leaving only the component you want.

The community calls this class of operation inter-axis value transfer. Its value is this: a 3D direction can be decomposed into components handled separately, which generalizes simple ratio points into projection and stretching. rotated ~ 0 appears three times in the cube frame in Chapter 5, each time doing the same job — "keep only yaw so the movement runs along a horizontal axis."

A companion rule

Once you insert a rotated, the original compensation distance is usually wrong. The positioned ~ ~-50000 ~ in 3.1 exists to cancel the height dropped along the hypotenuse; if you slip a rotated ~ 0 in the middle, the hypotenuse becomes a horizontal line, the drop changes, and the compensation distance must be recomputed.

So when modifying a command, don't change just one place. Go back to the action-list level and re-derive the whole chain.

Everything in this chapter:

Chapter 4　Advanced#

Everything in the first three chapters involved a single facing. From here on we chain multiple facings together.

The change chaining brings is qualitative: one facing can only do ratios; two can do a mirror; four can do a rotation. Paired with carefully designed distances, it can also compute squares, products, and quotients.

4.1 Far-point reflection#

This is the core primitive of all advanced CCT. Master it and every figure in Chapter 5 becomes readable.

Goal

Mirror a point about some line. The line passes through the origin, and its direction is given by some entity's rotation.

Skeleton

Command

Prepare three entities: the point to mirror P, the origin O, and axis, which supplies the axis direction.

If the origin is fixed at the world origin, you can just write facing 0 0 0 and drop one entity.

Why this is a mirror

Start with a simplified case to build intuition.

Let the axis be the Z axis (axis has yaw = 0, pitch = 0), and let point P sit to the right of the Z axis at distance w from it.

Step one walks H along the Z axis, arriving at a distant point A. Because H is large, A is essentially on the Z axis — P's small offset is negligible compared to H.

Step two faces the origin. Seen from A, the origin lies essentially along the negative Z direction. Note the word "essentially": because P has offset w, that direction carries a tiny tilt, with tilt angle about w/H.

Step three walks 2H. The tilt is amplified by 2H, producing a lateral displacement of about 2w, directed from P's side toward the far side.

Step four restores the axis direction, and step five walks H back.

Net effect: the component along the axis is preserved exactly (walked H out, walked H back), and the component perpendicular to the axis is flipped. That's a mirror.

Rigorous form

Let the origin be at (0,0,0), the point be the vector \mathbf{p}, and the axis unit vector be \mathbf{u}. Executing the five steps in order, the final position is:

\mathbf{p}_H = \mathbf{p} + 2H\mathbf{u} - 2H\frac{\mathbf{p}+H\mathbf{u}}{|\mathbf{p}+H\mathbf{u}|}

Section 6.3 gives the full derivation. As H goes to infinity this approaches:

\mathbf{p}' = 2(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{p})\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{p}

That's the standard axis-mirror formula: the component parallel to the axis is preserved, the perpendicular component is negated.

Error

The leading error is of order:

O\!\left(\frac{|\mathbf{p}|^2}{H}\right)

Same form as E_y \approx tD^2/2H from Chapter 2.

One decisive property

Mirroring doesn't change the point's distance from the origin:

|\mathbf{p}'| = |\mathbf{p}|

The parallel component keeps its length and the perpendicular component is merely negated, so a quick Pythagoras shows the magnitude is unchanged.

This property is the entire basis for drawing circles in 5.3.

4.2 Two reflections equal one rotation#

Result

Composing mirrors about two intersecting lines equals a rotation about their intersection. The rotation angle is twice the angle between the lines.

Think of mirrors: with two mirrors at 30 degrees, an object's image inside rotates 60 degrees.

Why twice

Let the angle between the two axes be θ. Take a point making angle α with the first axis.

After the first mirror, its angle with the first axis becomes -α. Its angle with the second axis is then -\alpha-\theta.

After the second mirror, its angle with the second axis becomes \alpha+\theta, i.e. \alpha+2\theta relative to the first axis.

Net rotation relative to the original position is 2\theta, independent of α. So no matter where the starting point is, it rotates by the same angle.

Command

Prepare two axis entities: axisA with yaw = 0, axisB with yaw = 30, both with pitch 0. Origin fixed at the world origin.

The structure is just two copies of the 4.1 skeleton back to back.

To move a position you have to compose displacements, and reflection composition is the shortest route: one reflection takes three positioneds, two take six, and you get rotation by an arbitrary angle in return.

That's why the H, 2H, H triplet appears everywhere in the figures of Chapter 5.

4.3 Squaring#

Now a change of direction: not geometric transforms, but arithmetic.

The idea

Pythagoras gives \sqrt{a^2+x^2}. Rearrange it:

\frac{a^2}{\sqrt{a^2+x^2}} = \frac{a^2}{a\sqrt{1+x^2/a^2}} = a\left(1+\frac{x^2}{a^2}\right)^{-1/2} \approx a - \frac{x^2}{2a}

Look at that last expression: a quantity containing \sqrt{a^2+x^2}, expanded, naturally produces a term proportional to x^2. Construct that quantity, subtract off the constant leading term, and what's left is a multiple of x^2.

Formula (Appendix B.1)

Function: (x,y,z) \to (x,\ y+x^2,\ z)

Derivation

Let the current position be (x,y,z).

facing 0 ~a ~ points at (0,\ y+a,\ z), so the direction vector is (-x,\ a,\ 0) with length s=\sqrt{a^2+x^2}. The unit direction:

\mathbf{F} = \frac{(-x,\ a,\ 0)}{s}

positioned ^ ^ ^-a² walks -a^2, a displacement of \left(\dfrac{a^2x}{s},\ -\dfrac{a^3}{s},\ 0\right).

rotated ~180 ~ adds 180 degrees to yaw. This step reverses only the horizontal components, leaving Y unchanged, so \mathbf{F} becomes \dfrac{(x,\ a,\ 0)}{s}.

Walking -a^2 again gives a displacement of \left(-\dfrac{a^2x}{s},\ -\dfrac{a^3}{s},\ 0\right).

Summing the two: X cancels completely, Y accumulates -\dfrac{2a^3}{s}.

Finally positioned ~ ~2a² ~ adds a constant. Total change in Y:

2a^2 - \frac{2a^3}{s} = 2a^2 - 2a^2\left(1+\frac{x^2}{a^2}\right)^{-1/2} \approx 2a^2 - 2a^2\left(1-\frac{x^2}{2a^2}\right) = x^2

Both x and z are preserved exactly, and y has increased by x^2.

What that rotated ~180 ~ is for

It makes the horizontal components of the two movements cancel and the vertical components add. This "flip and walk again" device is all over the appendix formula tables; when you see it, think "some component is about to be eliminated."

A shorter version (Appendix B.2)

Only three subcommands, at the cost of destroying the X coordinate. The function becomes (x,y,z) \to (?,\ y+x^2,\ z).

Error

The next dropped term is O(x^4/a^2). Error grows with input x, so always scale the input down to a small interval before use; [-1,1] is a common choice. Use the geometric scaling formulas in Appendix A.3 or A.4 for the scaling.

4.4 Multiplication#

With squaring in hand, multiplication follows immediately. The difference of squares:

xz = \frac{(x+z)^2 - (x-z)^2}{4}

Verify by expanding: (x^2+2xz+z^2)-(x^2-2xz+z^2) = 4xz.

So in principle two squarings, one subtraction, and one division by 4 will do. This route is conceptually clearest; the drawback is command length.

The multiplication versions in the appendix take a different route: one normalization of a 2D vector's hypotenuse does it all. The shortest uses only six subcommands (B.5):

Function: (x,y,z) \to (?,\ y+xz,\ ?)

That rotated ~45 ~ and the \sqrt2 are a matched pair: \cos 45° = 1/\sqrt2. The constants in formulas like this stand in strict correspondence — change one and you must change the other.

4.5 Division and higher powers#

Division

The normalization facing performs is itself a division:

\frac{(x,y,z)}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}}

The idea behind the division formulas is to arrange suitable auxiliary axes in advance so that the normalization denominator equals exactly the divisor, then read the quotient off one component of the unit direction.

The shortest version (Appendix B.10):

Function: (x,y,z) \to (?,\ ?,\ x/z)

Here a is a large number and b is a small one; together they squeeze the normalization denominator down to just the divisor term. If the parameters aren't at the right magnitudes, the formula doesn't hold.

A hard limit on division

As the divisor approaches 0, the error grows without bound. That isn't bad formula design — it's a property of 1/z itself. Using a division formula requires restricting the input domain so the divisor stays well away from 0.

Higher powers

With multiplication you can cascade:

Each cascade level adds both error and command length. Fourth power is about the practical ceiling.

4.6 Error engineering#

Error has come up repeatedly; this section organizes how to handle it.

Two kinds of error

Geometric truncation error comes from \sqrt{H^2+D^2}\approx H:

E_g \approx C_1\frac{r^2}{H}

Floating-point error comes from finite precision:

E_f \approx C_2\varepsilon H

One shrinks as H grows, the other grows.

Sweeping H

When sweeping, note that far-point reflection uses H, 2H, H in three places and they must be replaced together. Changing only the middle 2H breaks the formula outright.

If error keeps dropping as H grows, geometric error still dominates and you can push higher. If error rises after you increase H, floating-point error has taken over and you should back off.

Cover the boundaries

Arithmetic formulas must be tested against: positive numbers, negative numbers, 0, very small divisors, both endpoints of the input domain, different quadrants, different pitches.

A formula that only works for small positive numbers can't be used as a general-purpose formula.

Chapter 5　Applications#

The toolkit is sufficient. This chapter assembles it into cube frames, circles, and ellipses.

Assembly follows one uniform pattern: generate a small batch of sample points, then use reflections to copy and rotate them into every orientation. Once you understand this pattern, those several-hundred-character community commands all come apart cleanly.

5.1 Binary weights#

Goal

Generate 2^k evenly distributed points on a line segment.

The idea

Each layer offers two choices: "add d" or "subtract d." k layers give 2^k combinations. If d decreases in binary fashion:

1,\ 0.5,\ 0.25,\ \ldots

then the sums of these combinations tile an interval evenly.

Verification

Take three layers with weights 1, 0.5, 0.25. All combinations are \pm1\pm0.5\pm0.25, giving eight values:

-1.75,\ -1.25,\ -0.75,\ -0.25,\ 0.25,\ 0.75,\ 1.25,\ 1.75

Every adjacent gap is 0.5 — perfectly even, spanning [-1.75,\ 1.75].

General formula

To generate 2^k evenly spaced points on [-L,\ L], the weight of layer j is:

w_j = L\cdot\frac{2^{k-1-j}}{2^k-1}, \qquad j=0,1,\ldots,k-1

The weights sum to exactly L. The spacing between adjacent points:

\Delta = \frac{2L}{2^k-1}

Substituting L=1.75, k=3:

w_0 = 1.75\times\frac47 = 1, \qquad w_1 = 1.75\times\frac27 = 0.5, \qquad w_2 = 1.75\times\frac17 = 0.25

Consistent with the example above.

Why this formula is valuable

It lets you freely adjust figure size and sampling density. To double the figure's size, double L and double every weight. To double the sample points per edge, add one to k and recompute the weights.

Changing an individual weight arbitrarily, off-formula, destroys the evenness.

5.3 Circle: angle bisection#

There are two approaches to drawing a circle; we'll start with the one that follows most directly from what came before.

The idea

As 4.2 showed, two reflections equal one rotation. If each layer uses two different axes, one starting point splits into two directions; layer by layer, the number of directions grows as a power of 2.

Pair that with a halving sequence of angles:

45°,\ 22.5°,\ 11.25°,\ 5.625°,\ 2.8125°,\ 1.40625°

that is, 45°/2^j. Six layers give 2^6=64 directions. The angular step of an ideal circle is exactly:

\frac{360°}{64} = 5.625°

Why the radius is exactly 2

The start is positioned 0 2 0, a distance of 2 from the origin.

Recall the property from the end of 4.1: mirroring doesn't change the distance from the origin. This command contains nothing but reflections — no other displacement — so after every step:

|\mathbf{p}| = 2

Points always 2 from the origin lie on a sphere of radius 2. And because every axis has yaw 0 or 180 (X component zero), the points stay in the X = 0 plane. Sphere meets plane, giving a circle of radius 2.

The angle bisection ensures those 64 points are roughly even around the circle.

Changing the radius

Change the starting 0 2 0 to 0 R 0; no other number needs touching. That's this method's biggest convenience.

Changing the layer count

One fewer layer gives 32 points, one more gives 128. Just continue halving the angle sequence (the next term is 0.703125).

Chapter 6　Extension: Algebraic Expression of CCT#

The first five chapters used geometric intuition: similar triangles, mirrors, inscribed angles. That language is easy to grasp, but it has a limitation — faced with a combination you haven't seen, all you can do is guess.

This chapter switches to algebraic language. Write each subcommand as a mathematical transform, and any long command can be computed segment by segment. If you want to design new formulas yourself, this is the required tool.

This chapter uses vectors, matrices, and trigonometry. If you only want to draw figures with CCT, the first five chapters are enough.

6.1 State and the rotation matrix#

The state of an execute chain at any moment is:

\text{State} = (\mathbf{P},\ \mathbf{R})
  • \mathbf{P}: the execution position vector (x,y,z)
  • \mathbf{R}: the rotation matrix, determined by the rotation (ry,\ rx)

This chapter uses ry for yaw and rx for pitch.

The rotation matrix

\mathbf{R} has three orthonormal vectors as its columns:

\mathbf{R} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{L} & \mathbf{U} & \mathbf{F}\end{bmatrix}
VectorMeaningCorresponding ^ argument
\mathbf{L}Leftfirst
\mathbf{U}Upsecond
\mathbf{F}Frontthird

All three are mutually perpendicular, and \mathbf{L} = \mathbf{U}\times\mathbf{F}.

Expanded:

\mathbf{R} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos ry & -\sin rx\sin ry & -\cos rx\sin ry \\ 0 & \cos rx & -\sin rx \\ \sin ry & \sin rx\cos ry & \cos rx\cos ry \end{bmatrix}

The three columns are:

\mathbf{L} = (\cos ry,\ 0,\ \sin ry)
\mathbf{U} = (-\sin rx\sin ry,\ \cos rx,\ \sin rx\cos ry)
\mathbf{F} = (-\cos rx\sin ry,\ -\sin rx,\ \cos rx\cos ry)

Two immediately usable observations

First, the Y component of \mathbf{L} is always 0. The left vector is always horizontal, independent of pitch. That's why the local left shift in 5.2 moves cleanly along the X axis.

Second, the Y component of \mathbf{F} contains only rx, independent of ry. So changing yaw doesn't affect the height component of the front direction — both the seed segment in 5.2 and the ellipse transform in 5.5 are built on this.

6.3 Rigorous derivation of far-point reflection#

Now let's redo section 4.1 with algebraic tools and see where that expression rigorously comes from.

Setup: origin at (0,0,0), current point \mathbf{p}, axis unit vector \mathbf{u}, large number H.

Step 1, rotated as axis: \mathbf{F}=\mathbf{u}

Step 2, positioned ^ ^ ^H:

\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{p}+H\mathbf{u}

Step 3, facing 0 0 0:

\mathbf{v} = -(\mathbf{p}+H\mathbf{u}), \qquad \mathbf{F} = -\frac{\mathbf{p}+H\mathbf{u}}{|\mathbf{p}+H\mathbf{u}|}

Step 4, positioned ^ ^ ^2H:

\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{p}+H\mathbf{u}-2H\frac{\mathbf{p}+H\mathbf{u}}{|\mathbf{p}+H\mathbf{u}|}

Step 5, rotated as axis: \mathbf{F}=\mathbf{u}

Step 6, positioned ^ ^ ^H:

\boxed{\ \mathbf{p}_H = \mathbf{p}+2H\mathbf{u}-2H\dfrac{\mathbf{p}+H\mathbf{u}}{|\mathbf{p}+H\mathbf{u}|}\ }

Taking the limit

Decompose \mathbf{p}:

\mathbf{p} = s\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}, \qquad s=\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{p}, \qquad \mathbf{w}\perp\mathbf{u}

Then \mathbf{p}+H\mathbf{u} = (H+s)\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}, and

|\mathbf{p}+H\mathbf{u}| = \sqrt{(H+s)^2+|\mathbf{w}|^2} \approx (H+s)+\frac{|\mathbf{w}|^2}{2(H+s)}

Substitute back and take the leading term as H\to\infty. Note that \dfrac{2H}{H+s}\to2:

\mathbf{p}_\infty = s\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}+2H\mathbf{u}-2H\mathbf{u}-2\mathbf{w} = s\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{w}

That is,

\mathbf{p}' = 2(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{p})\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{p}

Matrix form:

S_\mathbf{u} = 2\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^T-I

The component parallel to the axis is preserved, the perpendicular one flipped — exactly the conclusion reached by geometric intuition in 4.1.

Error magnitude

The dropped terms are of order

O\!\left(\frac{|\mathbf{w}|^2}{H}\right) = O\!\left(\frac{|\mathbf{p}|^2}{H}\right)

Consistent with the form in Chapter 2.

Why distance is preserved

|\mathbf{p}'|^2 = |s\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{w}|^2 = s^2+|\mathbf{w}|^2 = |\mathbf{p}|^2

using \mathbf{u}\perp\mathbf{w}, so the cross term is 0.

This is the entire basis for drawing circles in 5.3: the start is 2 from the origin, and no number of reflections changes that.

6.4 What kind of transform is a reflection, really#

The properties of S_\mathbf{u} = 2\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^T-I are worth seeing clearly.

Eigenvalues: vectors along \mathbf{u} map to themselves (eigenvalue +1), and the two directions perpendicular to \mathbf{u} are negated (eigenvalue -1, multiplicity 2).

Determinant:

\det S_\mathbf{u} = (+1)\times(-1)\times(-1) = +1

Determinant +1 and orthogonal means it is a rotation — specifically, a 180-degree rotation about the axis \mathbf{u}.

This is worth noting: in three dimensions, "symmetry about a line" and "rotation by half a turn about that line" are the same transform. Earlier chapters used mirrors for intuitive explanation; algebraically the more accurate statement is a 180-degree rotation. A true mirror reflection, with determinant -1, is I-2\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^T (where \mathbf{n} is the plane normal), and CCT cannot produce that, because it changes handedness.

Composition

R = S_\mathbf{v}S_\mathbf{u}

The composition of two rotations is still a rotation. The result:

\text{rotation axis} = \text{direction of }\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}, \qquad \text{rotation angle} = 2\theta

where \theta is the angle between the axes. That's the source of the angle table in 4.2.

Rodrigues' rotation formula

Useful when designing new rotation combinations:

\mathbf{v}' = \mathbf{v}\cos\theta+(\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{v})\sin\theta+\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{v})(1-\cos\theta)

Equivalent form:

\mathbf{v}' = \mathbf{v}+(\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{v})\sin\theta+\mathbf{k}\times(\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{v})(1-\cos\theta)

\mathbf{k} must be a unit vector.

Check by substituting \theta=180°: \cos\theta=-1, \sin\theta=0, giving

\mathbf{v}' = -\mathbf{v}+2\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{v}) = 2(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{v})\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{v}

Exactly S_\mathbf{k}. The two routes agree.

6.5 Back to in-game angles#

Derivations produce \sin ry, \cos ry, \sin rx, \cos rx, but commands need angles. Convert with atan2:

ry = \text{atan2}(\sin ry,\ \cos ry)\times\frac{180}{\pi}
rx = \text{atan2}(\sin rx,\ \cos rx)\times\frac{180}{\pi}

Use atan2 rather than arctan, because arctan's range covers only half a turn and can't distinguish the second and third quadrants. atan2 takes two arguments and can give a full turn.

Units

atan2 returns radians; the angle arguments in commands are degrees. Don't drop the factor of 180/\pi. Mixing degrees and radians is the most common source of error in derivations like these. A recommended convention: use radians throughout the math, and convert to degrees once at the end before writing the command.

6.6 The limits of the algebraic approach#

The formulas in this chapter give theoretical values.

Several layers still sit between theory and practice: floating-point precision, how angles are represented internally by the game, numeric parsing, version differences. None of these fall within what the algebraic model describes.

So the correct use of the algebraic approach is to design and validate ideas, not to produce final parameters.

A practical workflow:

Step 2 deserves emphasis. Many formulas are perfect as H\to\infty but have unusable error at realistic H. Working it out once on paper or on a computer is far faster than repeated in-game trial and error.

Two conclusions to remember:

  • All nonlinearity comes from the normalization denominator |\mathbf{v}| in facing
  • 2\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^T-I is a 180-degree rotation about \mathbf{u}, and composing two of them gives a rotation of 2\theta

With this toolkit you can do what the first five chapters couldn't: take an unfamiliar long command and compute its effect segment by segment; or, in reverse, write down the transformation matrix you want and decompose it into a sequence of subcommands.

E　Common angles and constants#

E.1　Angle-halving sequence#

\theta_j = \frac{45°}{2^j}

Used for circles, regular polygons, stars, and other rotationally symmetric figures. k layers give 2^k directions with an angular step of 360°/2^k.

E.2　Binary weights#

To generate 2^k evenly spaced points on [-L,\ L], the weight of layer j is:

w_j = L\cdot\frac{2^{k-1-j}}{2^k-1}, \qquad j=0,1,\ldots,k-1

The weights sum to exactly L. Spacing between adjacent points:

\Delta = \frac{2L}{2^k-1}

For L=1.75, k=3: w_0=1, w_1=0.5, w_2=0.25.

E.4　H and error#

E(H) \approx C_1\frac{r^2}{H}+C_2\varepsilon H

The first term is geometric truncation error (smaller as H grows), the second is floating-point error (larger as H grows). Total error is minimized when the two are equal:

H \sim \frac{r}{\sqrt\varepsilon}

Estimating with single precision, \varepsilon\approx2^{-23}, gives H\sim2900\,r.

This explains the community's usual 1024, 2048, 4096, 16384, 32768.

Bigger H is not always better. When changing H, every related distance in the formula must be replaced together — far-point reflection uses H, 2H, H in three places, and changing only one breaks the formula.

k binary layers give 2^k final points. The per-layer choice counts multiply, they don't add.

Closing#

Everything in CCT comes down to four actions:

ActionPurpose
positionedchange the virtual execution point
rotatedchange the local coordinate basis
facingnormalize a vector into a heading
the large number Hreduce a nonlinear normalization to a controllable linear or quadratic relation

From these four actions you can construct ratio points, mirrors, rotations, scaling, projection, squaring, multiplication and division, dot products, cross products — and from there draw circles, ellipses, cube frames, and all kinds of symmetric figures.

One bottom line to keep in mind always:

The vast majority of advanced CCT formulas are asymptotic approximations under finite precision, not algebraic identities at infinite precision.

Understand that, and you can make the tradeoff between error and performance, turning CCT from "a long command that runs" into a coordinate computation system that is derivable, maintainable, and extensible.
