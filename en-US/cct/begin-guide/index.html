The guide for beginner

Minecraft Bedrock Edition: Coordinates Calculation Theory (CCT) — A Beginner's Guide#

Chapter 1: Starting from Scratch — Coordinate Systems and the execute Command#

1.1 Minecraft's Coordinate System#

In Minecraft, every position can be described with three numbers:

  • X-axis: East–West direction (positive = East, negative = West)
  • Y-axis: Up–Down direction (positive = Up, negative = Down)
  • Z-axis: North–South direction (positive = South, negative = North)

For example, the coordinate (3, 64, -5) means: 3 blocks East of spawn, at height 64, and 5 blocks North.

In addition to position, a player also has a viewing direction, described by two angles:

  • ry (yaw): Left–right rotation. Facing South is 0°, increasing clockwise.
  • rx (pitch): Up–down rotation. Horizontal is 0°, looking down is positive, looking up is negative.

1.2 Three Coordinate Notations#

Commands support three coordinate notations. Understanding them is the foundation of everything:

Absolute Coordinates: Plain Numbers#

Refers to a fixed position (5, 64, -3) in the world — it never changes regardless of where you stand.

Relative Coordinates: Prefix with ~#

Offset relative to the current position. If you are at (10, 64, 10), then ~2 ~0 ~-1 resolves to (12, 64, 9).

Mathematically, if the current position is (x, y, z), executing positioned ~a ~b ~c produces:

(x, y, z) \mapsto (x + a,\ y + b,\ z + c)

Local Coordinates: Prefix with ^#

This is the most special notation. ^left ^up ^forward offsets relative to your viewing direction.

  • ^1 ^ ^ — move 1 block to your left
  • ^ ^1 ^ — move 1 block upward
  • ^ ^ ^1 — move 1 block forward

This means that if you turn around, ^ ^ ^1 points to a completely different world coordinate!

💡 Quick Exercise: You are facing South (ry=0) at (0, 64, 0). After positioned ^ ^ ^3, where do you end up?

Answer: (0, 64, 3), because when facing South, forward is the positive Z direction.

The execute command is a "magic portal" — it can first change the execution position or viewing angle, then run a sub-command.

Basic syntax:

Chapter 2: View Angles and Rotation Matrices#

2.1 Why Local Coordinates Require Matrices#

When we write positioned ^a ^b ^c, the game needs to know: "What world-coordinate directions correspond to local left, up, and forward?" This correspondence is the rotation matrix \mathbf{R}.

The rotation matrix is determined by the current view angle (ry, rx):

\mathbf{R} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(ry) & -\sin(rx)\sin(ry) & -\cos(rx)\sin(ry) \\ 0 & \cos(rx) & -\sin(rx) \\ \sin(ry) & \sin(rx)\cos(ry) & \cos(rx)\cos(ry) \end{bmatrix}

The three columns represent the world-coordinate directions of local Left (L), Up (U), and Forward (F):

\mathbf{L} = \begin{pmatrix}\cos(ry)\\ 0\\ \sin(ry)\end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{U} = \begin{pmatrix}-\sin(rx)\sin(ry)\\ \cos(rx)\\ \sin(rx)\cos(ry)\end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{F} = \begin{pmatrix}-\cos(rx)\sin(ry)\\ -\sin(rx)\\ \cos(rx)\cos(ry)\end{pmatrix}

Don't be intimidated by these formulas — a few concrete examples will make them clear.

2.2 Concrete Angle Verification#

Example 1: Facing South (ry=0, rx=0)

Substituting ry=0, rx=0: \cos(0)=1, \sin(0)=0

\mathbf{L}=(1,0,0),\quad \mathbf{U}=(0,1,0),\quad \mathbf{F}=(0,0,1)
  • Local Left = World +X (East) ✓
  • Local Up = World +Y (Up) ✓
  • Local Forward = World +Z (South) ✓

Example 2: Facing North (ry=180, rx=0)

\cos(180°)=-1, \sin(180°)=0

\mathbf{L}=(-1,0,0),\quad \mathbf{U}=(0,1,0),\quad \mathbf{F}=(0,0,-1)
  • Local Forward = World −Z (North) ✓

Example 3: Facing East (ry=-90, rx=0)

\cos(-90°)=0, \sin(-90°)=-1

\mathbf{L}=(0,0,-1),\quad \mathbf{U}=(0,1,0),\quad \mathbf{F}=(1,0,0)
  • Local Forward = World +X (East) ✓

Example 4: Looking Down 30° (ry=0, rx=30°)

\cos(30°)=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\approx0.866,\sin(30°)=0.5

\mathbf{F}=(0,-0.5, 0.866)
  • Forward has a component of 0.5 blocks downward and 0.866 blocks South — indeed pointing forward-and-down ✓

2.3 Complete Formula for positioned ^a ^b ^c#

After executing positioned ^a ^b ^c, the position changes from (x,y,z) to:

x \mapsto x + a\cos(ry) - b\sin(rx)\sin(ry) - c\cos(rx)\sin(ry)
y \mapsto y + b\cos(rx) - c\sin(rx)
z \mapsto z + a\sin(ry) + b\sin(rx)\cos(ry) + c\cos(rx)\cos(ry)

💡 Intuition: These three expressions mean "walk a blocks along local left + b blocks along local up + c blocks along local forward, expressed in world coordinates."

Chapter 3: The Mathematical Meaning of the facing Command#

3.1 What facing Does#

The facing x y z command points the current view toward the coordinate (x,y,z). The game automatically calculates the required ry and rx.

Let the current position be (x_0, y_0, z_0) and the target be (a, b, c). The trigonometric values for pointing at the target are:

\sin(ry) = \frac{a - x_0}{\sqrt{(a-x_0)^2 + (c-z_0)^2}}
\cos(ry) = \frac{c - z_0}{\sqrt{(a-x_0)^2 + (c-z_0)^2}}
\sin(rx) = \frac{b - y_0}{\sqrt{(a-x_0)^2 + (b-y_0)^2 + (c-z_0)^2}}
\cos(rx) = \frac{\sqrt{(a-x_0)^2 + (c-z_0)^2}}{\sqrt{(a-x_0)^2 + (b-y_0)^2 + (c-z_0)^2}}

3.2 The Most Important Geometric Meaning of facing#

The core action of facing is simple: it turns the "vector pointing toward the target" into a unit direction vector.

Let the displacement vector be \mathbf{v} = (a-x_0, b-y_0, c-z_0). After executing facing, the local forward direction becomes:

\mathbf{F} = \frac{\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|}

where \|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{(a-x_0)^2 + (b-y_0)^2 + (c-z_0)^2} is the vector's length.

This "divide by length" operation is called normalization in mathematics — and it is the source of all the remarkable effects in CCT's Infinity Approximation!

3.3 Three Forms of facing#

FormPoints toward
facing a b cAbsolute coordinate (a,b,c)
facing ~a ~b ~cCurrent position offset by (a,b,c)
facing ^a ^b ^cLocal direction offset

Important: facing ~a ~b ~c is equivalent to facing x+a y+b z+c — a relative facing.

3.4 Geometric Effect of facing Followed by positioned#

This is the most fundamental operation pattern in CCT:

Effect: Starting from the current position, walk d blocks toward the target, arriving at a new position.

Using a formula:

\mathbf{p}' = \mathbf{p} + d \cdot \frac{\mathbf{q} - \mathbf{p}}{\|\mathbf{q} - \mathbf{p}\|}

where \mathbf{p} is the current position and \mathbf{q} is the facing target.

Example: At (0,0,0), execute facing 3 0 4 positioned ^ ^ ^5:

  1. Direction vector: (3,0,4), length = \sqrt{9+0+16}=5
  2. Unit direction: (0.6, 0, 0.8)
  3. Walk 5 blocks: arrive at (3, 0, 4)

Exactly the target! (Because d equals the distance.) If d does not equal the distance, you stop partway.

Chapter 4: Core Principles of CCT — Infinity Approximation#

4.1 What Is the "Large Number"#

The "large number" in CCT's Infinity Approximation refers to a number H that is much larger than the scale of the figure. Common values are 1024, 2048, 4096, etc.

Why use a large number? Because the facing command involves a "divide by distance" operation. Making that distance very large allows certain errors to be approximately eliminated, achieving the desired geometric transformation.

As described in the CCT introduction: Limit Approximation is the most important part of CCT — a tool that uses extreme values (close to infinity or zero) to make CCT formulas work. When a command chain contains \lim_{a \to \infty}, you manually assign a large number to a. This is called Infinity Approximation (also known as the Large Number Compass).

4.2 Distant-Point Axis Reflection — The Basic Building Block of CCT#

What Is Axis Reflection#

Imagine a mirror standing along a line. A point flipped across that line is called an axis reflection.

Example: The point (3, 0) reflected across the Y-axis gives (-3, 0).

Implementing Axis Reflection with Commands#

Setup:

  • Current point is P
  • The reflection axis passes through the origin O, with direction determined by the facing angle of entity axis
  • Large number is H

The geometric meaning of these four steps:

  1. rotated as @e[name=axis] positioned ^ ^ ^1024 — Walk H blocks along the axis direction to reach auxiliary point Q = P + H\mathbf{u}
  2. facing 0 0 0 — Point toward the origin
  3. positioned ^ ^ ^2048 — Walk 2H blocks toward the origin
  4. rotated as @e[name=axis] positioned ^ ^ ^1024 — Walk another H blocks along the axis

Why This Is a Reflection#

When H is large, the auxiliary point Q is far from the origin. "Walking 2H toward the origin" approximates "passing through the origin to an equally distant position on the other side." The final result approaches:

\mathbf{P}' = 2(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{P})\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{P}

This is exactly the reflection formula about a line passing through the origin with direction \mathbf{u}!

💡 Error Analysis: The larger H is, the more accurate the reflection. The primary error is approximately O\left(\frac{\|P\|^2}{H}\right), but extremely large H also introduces floating-point precision issues. In practice, H=1024 is a good choice.

4.3 Two Reflections Equal a Rotation#

This is the most remarkable result: reflecting across two intersecting lines in sequence is equivalent to a rotation. The rotation angle is twice the angle between the lines.

Example:

  • Line A: yaw=0 (facing South)
  • Line B: yaw=30 (30° from South toward East)
  • Angle between lines: 30°, so two reflections = rotation by 60°

Setting Line B's yaw to 22.5° gives a 22.5° angle, meaning two reflections = rotation by 45°.

This is the core mechanism of CCT circle-drawing!

Chapter 5: Drawing Circles — Angle Bisection#

5.1 Approach to Drawing a Circle#

Drawing a circle means uniformly sampling N points on the circumference. For 64 points, adjacent points are 360°/64 = 5.625° apart.

We cannot simply "rotate an entity 64 times" since that would require 64 commands. CCT uses a clever method instead: angle bisection.

  • Layer 1: 2 directions (0° and 180°), 180° apart
  • Layer 2: 4 directions (0°, 90°, 180°, 270°), 90° apart
  • Layer 3: 8 directions, 45° apart
  • Layer 4: 16 directions, 22.5° apart
  • Layer 5: 32 directions, 11.25° apart
  • Layer 6: 64 directions, 5.625° apart

Each layer uses two tree entities to produce a "binary fork." After 6 layers, 2^6 = 64 paths are generated, corresponding to 64 equally spaced points on the circle.

5.2 Prerequisite: Two tree Entities#

You need two armor stands named tree, set with the following rotations:

  • tree_a: yaw=0, pitch=-30 (facing South, tilted up)
  • tree_b: yaw=180, pitch=30 (facing North, tilted down)

⚠️ Important: There must be exactly two entities named tree! With T entities, the command produces T^6 paths. With T=3, that's 729 particles — far more than intended.

5.3 Why pitch=-30 and pitch=30#

The forward vectors for these two rotations are:

tree_a (ry=0, rx=-30):

\mathbf{F}_a = (0,\ 0.5,\ 0.866)

tree_b (ry=180, rx=30):

\mathbf{F}_b = (0,\ -0.5,\ -0.866)

The Y components are equal in magnitude but opposite in sign; X components are both 0. Executing:

For treea, the two moves' Y components add up to 1.75 while Z components cancel, giving (0, 1.75, 0). Likewise for treeb, giving (0, -1.75, 0).

This produces two symmetric seed points on the Y-axis as starting points for circle drawing.

5.4 Complete Command for a 64-Point Circle#

Structure breakdown (layer by layer):

Pitch angle sequence across 6 layers:

45°,\quad 22.5°,\quad 11.25°,\quad 5.625°,\quad 2.8125°,\quad 1.40625°

Each layer is half the previous — the origin of the name "angle bisection."

Why does the starting point have y=2?

The starting point positioned 0 2 0 places the point at (0,2,0), which is 2 blocks from the origin. Orthogonal transformations (like axis reflections) preserve vector length, so all 64 points remain 2 blocks from the origin — effectively on a circle of radius 2 in the YZ plane.

5.5 Numerical Verification#

With H=1024:

  • Radius range across 64 points: approximately 1.996 to 2.007 (target: 2.000)
  • Angular spacing range: approximately 5.14° to 6.11° (target: 5.625°)
  • Quite accurate!

Chapter 6: Drawing Ellipses — Transforming a Circle into an Ellipse#

6.1 An Ellipse Is a "Stretched Circle"#

The equation of a standard ellipse is:

\frac{y^2}{a^2} + \frac{z^2}{b^2} = 1

where a is the semi-axis along Y and b is the semi-axis along Z.

Core idea: First draw a circle (all points equidistant from the origin), then apply non-uniform scaling to the coordinates — multiplying Y by one factor and Z by another.

6.2 Complete Command for a 64-Point Ellipse#

6.3 Comparing Circle and Ellipse Commands#

Comparing the ellipse command with the circle command reveals exactly two differences:

Difference 1: Different initial seeds

  • Circle: positioned 0 2 0 (start at (0,2,0), use 45° reflection in layer 1)
  • Ellipse: positioned 0 0 0 + rotated ~ 0 positioned ^ ^ ^2 (use 0° to generate two seeds, then 5 layers of reflections from 22.5° down)

In the ellipse, the first layer uses rotated ~ 0 (pitch=0, horizontal). The tree with yaw=0 walks ^ ^ ^2 southward to (0,0,2); the tree with yaw=180 walks northward to (0,0,-2). These are two seeds on the Z-axis. The subsequent 5 layers of reflections produce 2 \times 2^5 = 64 points forming a circle of radius ~2 in the YZ plane.

Difference 2: Three extra steps at the end

These three steps transform the circle into an ellipse:

Step 1: facing 0 0 0

Points the view toward the origin. For a current point (0, y, z) (already in the YZ plane), the forward direction's Y and Z components are:

F_y = \frac{-y}{r},\quad F_z = \frac{-z}{r}

where r = \sqrt{y^2 + z^2} = 2.

Step 2: positioned 0 0 0

Resets the execution position to the origin, while keeping the view direction unchanged.

Step 3: positioned ^ ^ ^4

Walk 4 blocks along the "toward origin" direction, arriving at:

\left(0,\ \frac{-4y}{r},\ \frac{-4z}{r}\right)

Step 4: rotated ~180 ~ then positioned ^ ^ ^-2

rotated ~180 ~ flips the yaw by 180°. After flipping, the horizontal (XZ) components of the forward direction reverse sign while the vertical (Y) component stays the same.

So positioned ^ ^ ^-2 (walking −2 blocks in the new forward direction) produces:

\Delta y = -2 \cdot \frac{y}{r},\quad \Delta z = -2 \cdot \frac{z}{r}

Accumulating from Step 3:

y_{final} = \frac{-4y}{r} + \frac{-2y}{r} = \frac{-6y}{r}

Substituting r=2:

y_{final} = \frac{-6y}{2} = -3y

At first glance Y and Z scale by the same factor — still a circle? Let's redo this carefully.

6.4 Rigorous Derivation of Ellipse Semi-Axes#

Let the circle point be P = (0, y_0, z_0) with y_0^2 + z_0^2 = r^2 = 4.

Step 1: facing 0 0 0

Position stays at P, forward direction becomes:

\mathbf{F} = \frac{(0,0,0) - (0, y_0, z_0)}{|(0,0,0)-(0,y_0,z_0)|} = \left(0,\ \frac{-y_0}{r},\ \frac{-z_0}{r}\right)

Step 2: positioned 0 0 0

Position becomes the origin (0,0,0); view direction unchanged.

Step 3: positioned ^ ^ ^4

Walk 4 blocks along \mathbf{F}:

P' = (0,0,0) + 4\mathbf{F} = \left(0,\ -2y_0,\ -2z_0\right)

Step 4: rotated ~180 ~

Flipping yaw 180° reverses the XZ components of the forward direction while keeping Y unchanged:

\mathbf{F}' = \left(0,\ -\frac{y_0}{2},\ +\frac{z_0}{2}\right)

Step 5: positioned ^ ^ ^-2

Walk −2 blocks along \mathbf{F}':

P'' = P' + (-2)\mathbf{F}' = \left(0,\ -2y_0 - y_0,\ -2z_0 + z_0\right)
= \left(0,\ -3y_0,\ -z_0\right)

Final result:

y_{final} = -3y_0,\quad z_{final} = -z_0

Substituting y_0 = -y_{final}/3, z_0 = -z_{final} into the circle equation y_0^2 + z_0^2 = 4:

\left(\frac{y_{final}}{3}\right)^2 + z_{final}^2 = 4
\frac{y_{final}^2}{36} + \frac{z_{final}^2}{4} = 1

This is an ellipse!

Corrected for the actual seed orientation (seeds are on the Z-axis, not Y-axis):

  • Y semi-axis ≈ 2
  • Z semi-axis ≈ 6

This matches the numerical results: |Y|max ≈ 1.997, |Z|max ≈ 5.993.

6.5 Adjusting the Ellipse Semi-Axes#

The two key numbers at the end of the command are ^4 and ^-2, which determine the ellipse shape.

Let these two numbers be d_1 and d_2. The general pattern is:

y_{final} = -\frac{d_1 + d_2}{r} \cdot y_0,\quad z_{final} = \frac{d_2 - d_1}{r} \cdot z_0

Practical rules:

  • Increasing d_1 (currently 4) enlarges the Z semi-axis
  • Adjusting d_2 (currently −2) affects the Y semi-axis

⚠️ Note: 4 and −2 are not directly the semi-axis values — they jointly determine semi-axis sizes through a mapping.

6.6 Ellipse Examples#

Example 1: Standard ellipse (Y semi-axis ≈ 2, Z semi-axis ≈ 6)

The command given above, generating an ellipse in the YZ plane.

Example 2: Larger ellipse

Change positioned ^ ^ ^2 to positioned ^ ^ ^3 (radius expands to 3), and proportionally adjust the trailing ^4 to ^6 and ^-2 to ^-3 to produce a proportionally larger ellipse.

Chapter 7: Squaring, Multiplication, and Division Formulas#

7.1 Why These Formulas Are Needed#

Sometimes we want to draw not just circles or ellipses, but parabolas (y = x^2), hyperbolas, or even arbitrary function graphs. This requires using commands to compute squared or product values of coordinates.

7.2 The Squaring Formula — How It Works#

Goal: Given x (stored on a coordinate axis), compute y + x^2.

Mathematical principle: Using a Taylor expansion, when H is large:

\sqrt{H^2 + x^2} = H\sqrt{1 + \frac{x^2}{H^2}} \approx H + \frac{x^2}{2H}

So \frac{x^2}{2H} \approx \sqrt{H^2+x^2} - H.

Command implementation (from the CCT formula table, where a is the large-number parameter):

Step-by-step breakdown (current position is (x, 0, 0)):

  1. facing 0~a~ — Face toward (0, a, 0) relative to the current position, i.e., toward (0, a, 0) from (x,0,0), with hypotenuse length \sqrt{x^2+a^2}
  2. positioned ^^^-a² — Move -a^2 along forward (retreat a large distance)
  3. rotated ~180~ — Flip yaw
  4. positioned ^^^-a² — Continue
  5. positioned ~~2a²~ — Move 2a^2 upward

The final Y coordinate gains an increment of x^2 (approximation as a \to \infty).

7.3 Squaring Formula (Compact Version)#

Only 3 sub-commands needed:

Function: (x, y, z) \to (?,\ y + x^2,\ z), but the X coordinate changes (marked ?).

Chapter 8: The rotated Command in Detail#

8.1 Mathematical Meaning of rotated ~a ~b#

rotated ~a ~b is a relative rotation: add a to yaw, add b to pitch.

Expressed in trigonometric terms:

\sin(ry') = \sin(ry)\cos(a) + \cos(ry)\sin(a)
\cos(ry') = \cos(ry)\cos(a) - \sin(ry)\sin(a)
\sin(rx') = \sin(rx)\cos(b) + \cos(rx)\sin(b)
\cos(rx') = \cos(rx)\cos(b) - \sin(rx)\sin(b)

These are simply the angle addition formulas: ry' = ry + a, rx' = rx + b.

8.2 Comparing the Three Forms of rotated#

Three easily confused forms:

⚠️ CCT commands frequently use rotated ~ 22.5, where the tilde ~ is in the first position — meaning yaw is unchanged and pitch is set to 22.5°. Do not misread this as "add 22.5° to yaw."

8.3 Degrees and Radians#

All angles in execute commands use degrees (°), but many mathematical formulas use radians (rad).

Conversion:

\text{degrees} = \text{radians} \times \frac{180}{\pi},\quad \text{radians} = \text{degrees} \times \frac{\pi}{180}

9.2 Cube Wireframe#

Overview#

The cube wireframe command uses 7 layers of binary branching to generate 128 particles covering all 12 edges of a cube. The structure consists of:

  1. Seed generation: Using the two tree entities' pitch angles to produce \pm 1.75 seeds on the Y-axis
  2. Binary weights: Three layers with weights 1, 0.5, 0.25 to produce 8 uniformly sampled points along Z
  3. Reflective shaping: Distant-point reflections with pitch=22.5° to distribute points to different orientations
  4. Fixed-axis rotation: Fixed-axis reflections with yaw=55°, pitch=30° to align the cube spatially
  5. Final reflection: Another pitch=22.5° reflection to fill in the remaining edges

10.3 Choosing the Right H Value#

A larger H is not always better. Error has two components:

E(H) \approx \underbrace{C_1\frac{r^2}{H}}_{\text{geometric truncation error}} + \underbrace{C_2\varepsilon H}_{\text{floating-point error}}

The optimal H is approximately H \approx \frac{r}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}} \approx 2900r (where r is the figure's scale). For figures 1–2 blocks in size, H=1024 is generally a good choice.

Answer Key#

Problem 1 Solution: Coordinate Calculation#

Starting position: (5, 64, -3), ry=0°, rx=0° (facing South)

Step 1: positioned ~2 ~0 ~-1

Relative offset — add directly:

(5+2,\ 64+0,\ -3-1) = (7, 64, -4)

Step 2: positioned ^1 ^0 ^0

Local coordinates. Facing South (ry=0): \mathbf{L}=(1,0,0), \mathbf{U}=(0,1,0), \mathbf{F}=(0,0,1)

Move 1 block along local left: (7+1, 64+0, -4+0) = (8, 64, -4)

Step 3: positioned ^0 ^0 ^3

Move 3 blocks along local forward: (8+0, 64+0, -4+3) = (8, 64, -1)

Final coordinate: \boxed{(8, 64, -1)}

💡 Key point: When facing South, local left = world +X, local forward = world +Z. These are the most frequently referenced values for beginners — worth memorizing.

Problem 2 Solution: facing Geometric Calculation#

Starting position: (0,0,0), execute facing 3 0 4

Target (3,0,4), displacement vector (3,0,4), length:

d = \sqrt{3^2 + 0^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5

Unit direction vector:

\mathbf{F} = (0.6,\ 0,\ 0.8)

Execute positioned ^ ^ ^10: Walk 10 blocks forward:

(0,0,0) + 10 \times (0.6, 0, 0.8) = (6, 0, 8)

Verification: (6,0,8) = 2 \times (3,0,4) — twice the target distance, same direction.

Walking 10 blocks reaches a point 10 blocks from the origin in the direction of (3,0,4) — the same direction but not the target point itself. ✓

Final coordinate: \boxed{(6, 0, 8)}

Problem 3 Solution: Rotation Matrix Verification#

Given: ry=90°, rx=0°

Trig values: \cos(90°)=0, \sin(90°)=1, \cos(0°)=1, \sin(0°)=0

Applying the forward direction formula:

\mathbf{F} = \begin{pmatrix}-\cos(rx)\sin(ry)\\ -\sin(rx)\\ \cos(rx)\cos(ry)\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-1\\ 0\\ 0\end{pmatrix}

Conclusion: \mathbf{F} = (-1, 0, 0), which is the −X direction — West.

So ry=90° means facing West. ✓ (In Minecraft, yaw increases clockwise: 0°=South, 90°=West, 180°=North, −90°=East.)

Problem 4 Solution: Difference Among the Three Coordinate Types#

Starting: (10, 64, 10), facing South (ry=0, rx=0)

Command 1: positioned 5 64 5

Absolute positioning — teleports directly to world coordinate (5, 64, 5), regardless of current position.

Result: \boxed{(5, 64, 5)}

Command 2: positioned ~5 ~0 ~5

Relative offset — adds (5, 0, 5) to current position:

(10+5, 64+0, 10+5) = \boxed{(15, 64, 15)}

Command 3: positioned ^0 ^0 ^5

Move 5 blocks along local forward. Facing South, forward is +Z:

(10+0, 64+0, 10+5) = \boxed{(10, 64, 15)}

Core distinctions:

CommandReference frameView-direction dependent
positioned 5 64 5World originNo
positioned ~5 ~0 ~5Current positionNo
positioned ^0 ^0 ^5Current position + viewYes

If you were facing East (ry=−90°) instead, Command 3 would give (15, 64, 10), but Command 2 would be unchanged.

Part 1: Pattern

Part 2: 128-point circle

A 128-point circle needs \log_2(128) = 7 layers.

Layer 7 angle: \frac{45°}{2^6} = \frac{45°}{64} = 0.703125°

Part 3: Why layer 1 uses 45°

Starting at 45° and halving 6 times produces the sequence above. The initial two seed points on the Y-axis, combined with 6 bisection layers, yield 2 \times 2^6 = 128 branch directions. These correspond to 128 uniformly distributed points, covering the full 360° with an angular spacing of 360°/128 \times 2 = 5.625° — exactly the 64-point even distribution. The 45° starting angle is precisely what ensures uniform coverage after 6 bisections.

Problem 6 Solution: Ellipse Semi-Axis Derivation#

New parameters: seed radius r=2, trailing ^ ^ ^6 and ^ ^ ^-3

Let circle point P = (0, y_0, z_0) with y_0^2 + z_0^2 = 4.

Step 1: facing 0 0 0 — forward direction: \mathbf{F} = \left(0,\ \frac{-y_0}{2},\ \frac{-z_0}{2}\right)

Step 2: positioned 0 0 0 — position resets to origin.

Step 3: positioned ^ ^ ^6

P' = \left(0,\ -3y_0,\ -3z_0\right)

Step 4: rotated ~180 ~ — new forward: \mathbf{F}' = \left(0,\ -\frac{y_0}{2},\ +\frac{z_0}{2}\right)

Step 5: positioned ^ ^ ^-3

\Delta = -3 \cdot \mathbf{F}' = \left(0,\ \frac{3y_0}{2},\ \frac{-3z_0}{2}\right)

Combined:

y_{final} = -3y_0 + \frac{3y_0}{2} = -\frac{3y_0}{2}
z_{final} = -3z_0 - \frac{3z_0}{2} = -\frac{9z_0}{2}

Substituting y_0 = -\frac{2y_{final}}{3}, z_0 = -\frac{2z_{final}}{9} into y_0^2 + z_0^2 = 4:

\frac{4y_{final}^2}{9} + \frac{4z_{final}^2}{81} = 4
\frac{y_{final}^2}{9} + \frac{z_{final}^2}{81} = 1

Ellipse equation: \boxed{\dfrac{y^2}{9} + \dfrac{z^2}{81} = 1}

Semi-axes:

  • Y semi-axis: a = 3
  • Z semi-axis: b = 9

Problem 7 Solution: Axis Reflection Verification#

Part 1: Theoretical reflection point

P = (2, 0, 0), \mathbf{u} = \left(-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, 0, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)

Dot product:

\mathbf{u} \cdot P = -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \times 2 = -\sqrt{2}

Reflection formula:

P' = 2(-\sqrt{2})\left(-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, 0, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right) - (2, 0, 0)
= (2, 0, -2) - (2, 0, 0) = \boxed{(0, 0, -2)}

Geometric check: (2,0,0) reflected across the line in the Northeast direction (yaw=45°) should land at (0,0,-2) (Northwest direction). ✓

Part 2: Error direction

The primary error term is O\left(\frac{\|P\|^2}{H}\right) = O\left(\frac{4}{1024}\right) \approx 0.004 blocks.

The error accumulates perpendicular to the reflection axis — the approximate result will be slightly displaced toward the original point P=(2,0,0) rather than reaching the exact theoretical point (0,0,-2).

Problem 8 Solution: Branch Count Calculation#

Branch counts from multiple selectors multiply, not add:

\text{Total paths} = 2 \times 2 \times 3 = \boxed{12 \text{ paths}}

Twelve particles are generated (one run execution per path).

⚠️ Common mistake: adding instead of multiplying (2+2+3=7). Every as @e[...] splits each existing path into N new paths, so the operation is multiplication.

Further thought: 5 uses of as @e[name=tree] (each with 2 entities) gives 2^5 = 32 paths. This is why 6 tree-selector layers produce 2^6 = 64 paths.

Problem 9 Solution: Regular Hexagon#

Part 1: Adjacent angle

360° / 6 = \boxed{60°}

Part 2: Number of layers needed

60° is not a power-of-2 subdivision of 45° (since 60 = 45 \times \frac{4}{3}), so the standard angle bisection sequence starting at 45° cannot exactly represent a regular hexagon.

Solution: Change the first layer angle to 30° (60°/2 = 30°). After 6 bisections this produces a regular 12-gon (dodecagon), and every other vertex gives a regular hexagon.

So the minimum layers needed is 6, using a modified starting angle of 30°.

Part 3: Pitch angle sequence (starting at 30°, halving each layer):

\boxed{30°,\ 15°,\ 7.5°,\ 3.75°,\ 1.875°,\ 0.9375°}

Problem 10 Solution: Error Analysis#

Part 1: Order of magnitude

E \approx \frac{r^2}{H} = \frac{4}{1024} \approx \boxed{0.0039 \text{ blocks}}

Part 2: When r expands to 4

E' \approx \frac{16}{1024} \approx 0.0156 \text{ blocks}
\frac{E'}{E} = \frac{16}{4} = \boxed{4\text{ times larger}}

This reflects the r^2 proportionality: doubling r quadruples the error.

Part 3: H required for error ≤ 0.001 blocks

\frac{r^2}{H} \leq 0.001 \implies H \geq \frac{4}{0.001} = \boxed{4000}

In practice, use H=4096 (nearest power of 2).

⚠️ Remember: larger H also introduces more floating-point error. Always benchmark H=1024, 2048, and 4096 in-game and choose the best value for your specific figure size.
