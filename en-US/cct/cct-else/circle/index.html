How to draw a circle

Note: This document is written by senxyzplayeE.

How to Draw a Circle in Minecraft Bedrock Edition#

Let’s use the new /execute syntax as an example to explain a method. In the Minecraft Bedrock command community, this method is known as the "nested /execute dot-matrix circle".

Concept: To generate a circle, we can utilize the theorem that "the inscribed angle is half of the central angle subtended by the same arc." By repeatedly halving the angle and distributing the points along the arc, we can construct the circle.

Let A be the center of the circle, and B and C be the entities at the two ends of the circle's diameter. Let D be any point on the circle. Centered at A, extending outward by the radius distance in the directions of CD and BD will lead to points E and F. It is easy to see that these are the midpoints of arcs CD and BD, respectively.

Next, repeat the same operation on points E and F as we did for point D. This yields H, J, G, and I. It is easy to see that no matter where the starting point D is, the angle between these 4 points will always be 45 degrees.

Continuing this nested operation, you will find that more and more points split on the circle, and they are all uniformly distributed.

The detailed command is as follows, where a is the center, and r represents the two entities located at the ends of the diameter. Note that their placement must be at a distance of exactly R from the center a, otherwise the generated circle will not be uniformly distributed.

The part enclosed in curly brackets {} is the nested part. Enter this segment repeatedly; the more times it is repeated, the denser the generated points will be.

Notice that the nested part does not involve rotation calculations and is independent of x, y, z coordinates. This means we can change the orientation of the generated dot-matrix circle simply by changing the starting point's orientation:

Tilt the angle of the brackets by a certain degree (remember to ensure the distance is still R), and then adjust the rx (pitch) rotation to "stand" the circle up. This generates a tilted circle.

As you can imagine, with proper placement, a circle of any orientation can be generated.

However, this command has limitations: it can only generate perfect circles, not tilted ellipses. How can we draw truly arbitrary-oriented circles and ellipses?

Let's look at the parametric equation of a planar ellipse:

(x, y) = (a, b) \cos\theta + (c, d) \sin\theta

Where (a, b) and (c, d) are two perpendicular vectors, and \theta is the parameter.

Let's rewrite it:

\begin{aligned} x &= a \cos\theta + c \sin\theta \\ y &= b \cos\theta + d \sin\theta \end{aligned}

Using the auxiliary angle formula (harmonic addition theorem), we transform it again:

\begin{aligned} x &= A \sin(\theta + \varphi) \\ y &= B \sin(\theta + \psi) \end{aligned}

This shows that we can represent any ellipse by superimposing two sine waves in the x and y directions that have the same period but a certain phase difference.

Take a look at this linkage mechanism. Let AB be the rotation angle (yaw) of the rotating entity, and BC be its symmetrical angle. Both have a length of 2.5. DD'_1 and DC are also symmetrical, with a length of 1.5.

It is easy to calculate: when \angle xAB = \theta, the length of AC (blue line) is 5 \cos\theta, and the length of CD_1 (red line) is 3 \sin\theta. From this, we know that D_1 is the result of vertically superimposing two sine waves with amplitudes of 5 and 3, and a phase difference of \pi/2. Its trajectory is a standard ellipse with a semi-minor axis of 5 and a semi-major axis of 3.

Note that the vector \vec{AD_1} can be represented as:

\vec{AD_1} = \vec{AB} + \vec{BD} + \vec{DD_1} = \vec{AB} + \vec{DD_1} + \vec{BD}

Therefore, we can simplify this linkage model.

As shown in the figure, BA = 4, AA_1 = 1. The two angles remain symmetrical. This yields the same ellipse as before. It is not hard to see that if both BA and AA_1 rotate 45 degrees in the same direction, the entire ellipse will also rotate 45 degrees in that direction. If only AA_1 rotates 50 degrees in one direction, the entire ellipse will rotate 25 degrees in that direction.

With this principle, we can draw ellipses of any orientation within the horizontal plane in Minecraft.

So, how do we draw an ellipse of any orientation in 3D space? Following the same principle, in 3D space, we only need to superimpose three mutually perpendicular sine waves with equal periods and certain phase differences. The proof is omitted here.

With our previous experience, we can deduce that we only need three linkages to draw all ellipses in 3D space. Modifying the lengths and rotation angles will adjust the shape and orientation of the ellipse.

However, limited by the pitch (rx) characteristics of boats in Minecraft, the author had to split the ellipse-drawing command into two halves.

These two commands can represent all ellipses. By modifying the values of A, B, C, \varphi, and \psi, you can adjust the size, shape, and orientation of the ellipse.

If we want an ellipse with semi-major and semi-minor axes of (x_1, y_1, z_1) and (x_2, y_2, z_2) respectively, what values should we set for these 5 parameters?

Although mathematical methods can yield the corresponding calculations, the results are highly complex and involve complicated inverse trigonometric functions. To avoid these calculations, we can modify the linkage mechanism to make it simpler.

Let's look back at the parametric equation of a 2D ellipse:

(x, y) = (x_1, y_1) \cos\theta + (x_2, y_2) \sin\theta

Let's rewrite it and keep it at this step:

\begin{aligned} x &= x_1 \cos\theta + x_2 \sin\theta \\ y &= y_1 \cos\theta + y_2 \sin\theta \end{aligned}

Instead of combining them using trigonometric identities, we represent them directly.

As shown in the figure, let \angle xAB = \theta, taking the clockwise direction as positive. Let AB = BB_1 = x_1 / 2, and B_1C = CD = x_2 / 2. BB_1 is perpendicular to B_1C. It is very easy to prove that AD = x_1 \cos\theta and DB_1 = x_2 \sin\theta. At this point, the x-coordinate of point D is the value of x we want to represent.

By performing the same operation for y, we can draw any 2D ellipse. Notice that AB \parallel E'D'', BB_1 \parallel D'E', and B_1C \parallel ED'. By combining all parallel vectors, we can simplify this linkage even further. The angles in such a system do not contain parameters and are very easy to calculate.
