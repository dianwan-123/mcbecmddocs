About Polynomial

Note: Article by senxyzplayeE.

All techniques in Coordinates Calculation Theory (CCT) are rooted in a single subcommand: facing. The reason is that the algebraic form of facing contains a square root. Building upon this, command creators have leveraged various approximations to achieve a wide array of transformations.

Today, we will implement the transformation (x,y,z) \to (x, y + \sum p_n x^n, z). When considering \sum p_n x^n, Taylor expansion naturally comes to mind, as it rewrites most functions into polynomial form. Because the algebraic expression of facing contains a radical, we can theoretically compute polynomials by combining multiple facing subcommands.

Let us first assume the command representation of \sum p_n x^n (hereafter referred to as the polynomial P(x)) takes the following form, where the initial execution position is (x,y,z):

Thanks to rotated ~180~, this command sequence will not alter x or z upon completion; it only modifies y (as can also be proven via the Algebraic Expression of CCT).

It is straightforward to calculate the net increase in the y-coordinate:

\sum_{\substack{i \text{ is odd} \\ i \in [1, k]}} \frac{2a_i b_i}{\sqrt{a_i^2 + x^2}} + \sum_{\substack{i \text{ is even} \\ i \in [1, k]}} \frac{2x b_i}{\sqrt{a_i^2 + x^2}}

To establish the connection with polynomials, let us first analyze a single term:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{a_i^2 + x^2}} = \frac{1}{a_i}\left(1 + \frac{x^2}{a_i^2}\right)^{-1/2} = \sum_{m=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^m (2m)!}{4^m (m!)^2} \frac{x^{2m}}{a_i^{2m+1}}

Substituting the odd and even terms respectively yields the following identity:

f(x) = \sum_{m=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^m (2m)!}{4^m (m!)^2} \left[ \left(\sum_{i \text{ is odd}} \frac{2b_i}{a_i^{2m}} \right) x^{2m} + \left(\sum_{i \text{ is even}} \frac{2b_i}{a_i^{2m+1}} \right) x^{2m+1} \right]

Note that an exact solution is virtually impossible: the original function has singularities at x = \pm i a_i, whereas polynomials are entire functions. Consequently, except for the trivial case where all b_i=0, the two can never be exactly equal on any interval containing the origin. However, we can construct an approximation of arbitrarily high precision near x=0.

We can set up a system of linear equations such that the leading terms match the desired polynomial while higher-order terms tend toward 0. However, the system is underdetermined: we have 2n unknowns, but only n+1 target coefficients, meaning solutions are not unique. We can resolve these remaining degrees of freedom using a_1, a_2, \dots.

For convenience, we directly treat a_i as free variables by setting a_i = \alpha_i, where \alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots, \alpha_n are pairwise distinct parameters tending to positive infinity.

We aim to match the first n coefficients of the target polynomial P(x) (namely p_0 through p_{n-1}, letting p_n and beyond converge naturally via the limit process). Substituting a_i = \alpha_i into the Taylor expansion and matching coefficients yields the following systems of equations:

For even powers, for m = 0, 1, 2, \dots (\text{satisfying } 2m \le n-1):

\sum_{\substack{i \in [1, n] \\ i \text{ is odd}}} \frac{2b_i}{\alpha_i^{2m}} = \frac{4^m (m!)^2}{(-1)^m (2m)!} \cdot p_{2m}

For odd powers, for m = 0, 1, 2, \dots (\text{satisfying } 2m+1 \le n-1):

\sum_{\substack{i \in [1, n] \\ i \text{ is even}}} \frac{2b_i}{\alpha_i^{2m+1}} = \frac{4^m (m!)^2}{(-1)^m (2m)!} \cdot p_{2m+1}

For brevity, we express these in matrix form.
Suppose there are r odd terms (indexed i_1 = 1, i_2 = 3, \dots, i_r = 2r-1) and s even terms (indexed j_1 = 2, j_2 = 4, \dots, j_s = 2s).

Matrix for odd terms:

\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 & \cdots & 2 \\ \frac{2}{\alpha_{i_1}^2} & \frac{2}{\alpha_{i_2}^2} & \cdots & \frac{2}{\alpha_{i_r}^2} \\ \frac{2}{\alpha_{i_1}^4} & \frac{2}{\alpha_{i_2}^4} & \cdots & \frac{2}{\alpha_{i_r}^4} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{2}{\alpha_{i_1}^{2(r-1)}} & \frac{2}{\alpha_{i_2}^{2(r-1)}} & \cdots & \frac{2}{\alpha_{i_r}^{2(r-1)}} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} b_{i_1} \\ b_{i_2} \\ \vdots \\ b_{i_r} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} p_0 \\ -\frac{1}{2} p_2 \\ \frac{3}{8} p_4 \\ \vdots \\ \frac{(-1)^{r-1}(2r-2)!}{4^{r-1}[(r-1)!]^2} p_{2r-2} \end{bmatrix}

Matrix for even terms:

\begin{bmatrix} \frac{2}{\alpha_{j_1}} & \frac{2}{\alpha_{j_2}} & \cdots & \frac{2}{\alpha_{j_s}} \\ \frac{2}{\alpha_{j_1}^3} & \frac{2}{\alpha_{j_2}^3} & \cdots & \frac{2}{\alpha_{j_s}^3} \\ \frac{2}{\alpha_{j_1}^5} & \frac{2}{\alpha_{j_2}^5} & \cdots & \frac{2}{\alpha_{j_s}^5} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{2}{\alpha_{j_1}^{2s-1}} & \frac{2}{\alpha_{j_2}^{2s-1}} & \cdots & \frac{2}{\alpha_{j_s}^{2s-1}} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} b_{j_1} \\ b_{j_2} \\ \vdots \\ b_{j_s} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} p_1 \\ -\frac{1}{2} p_3 \\ \frac{3}{8} p_5 \\ \vdots \\ \frac{(-1)^{s-1}(2s-2)!}{4^{s-1}[(s-1)!]^2} p_{2s-1} \end{bmatrix}

Next, we present the explicit analytical solutions to these two matrix equations.

Definitions:
Odd index set: O = \{i \mid i \text{ is odd}, 1 \le i \le n\}, with |O| = r.
Even index set: E = \{i \mid i \text{ is even}, 1 \le i \le n\}, with |E| = s.

For the matrix of odd terms, for any odd index j \in O, the solution is:

b_j = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{m=0}^{r-1} \left[ \frac{4^m (m!)^2}{(-1)^m (2m)!} p_{2m} \right] \cdot \frac{(-1)^{r-1-m} e_{r-1-m}\left( \left\{ \frac{1}{\alpha_l^2} \;\middle|\; l \in O, l \ne j \right\} \right)}{\prod\limits_{l \in O, l \ne j} \left( \frac{1}{\alpha_j^2} - \frac{1}{\alpha_l^2} \right)}

where e_t(S) denotes the degree-t elementary symmetric polynomial on set S, with e_0(S) = 1.

For the matrix of even terms, for any even index j \in E, the solution is:

b_j = \frac{\alpha_j}{2} \sum_{m=0}^{s-1} \left[ \frac{4^m (m!)^2}{(-1)^m (2m)!} p_{2m+1} \right] \cdot \frac{(-1)^{s-1-m} e_{s-1-m}\left( \left\{ \frac{1}{\alpha_l^2} \;\middle|\; l \in E, l \ne j \right\} \right)}{\prod\limits_{l \in E, l \ne j} \left( \frac{1}{\alpha_j^2} - \frac{1}{\alpha_l^2} \right)}

If your goal is to be "just sufficient" (using the minimal k to exactly match coefficients while eliminating higher-order terms through the limit process, using only a single parameter tending to positive infinity to approximate a degree-\varphi polynomial), then the minimal k is simply \varphi + 1. The proof is omitted here.

Next, we prove that the remainder calculated via this method strictly tends to 0.
(As for why only a single parameter tending to positive infinity is required: we can set redundant variables to 0 or other fixed constants, as long as the system remains solvable.)

Before the proof, let us establish the notation conventions:

SymbolDescription
TUnique divergent parameter, T \to +\infty
\varphiDegree of the polynomial P(x)
c_iArbitrarily chosen fixed, pairwise distinct positive constants independent of T, used to define a_i = T \cdot c_i
\beta_iFixed real numbers solved from the first \varphi + 1 coefficient equations, independent of T, used to define b_i = T^\varphi \cdot \beta_i
RFixed domain radius
C_m(T)Coefficient of x^m in the Taylor expansion of the original function
S_mFixed constant independent of T, satisfying C_m(T) = T^{\varphi - m} \cdot S_m
\rho\max\limits_{1 \le i \le k} \frac{1}{c_i}, used to establish an upper bound on $\
BA fixed positive constant satisfying $\
R_T(x)Remainder sum over all higher-order terms (m \ge \varphi + 1)
qCommon ratio of the geometric series, satisfying q = (\rho R)/T

Setting: |x| \le R, a_i = T \cdot c_i, and b_i = T^\varphi \cdot \beta_i.

The remainder is given by:

R_T(x) = \sum_{m=\varphi+1}^\infty C_m(T) x^m

Bounding this expression:

|R_T(x)| \le \sum_{m=\varphi+1}^\infty T^{\varphi-m} |S_m| R^m \le B T^\varphi \sum_{m=\varphi+1}^\infty \left(\frac{\rho R}{T}\right)^m

Choose T > 2\rho R, and set q = \rho R / T < 1/2. Then:

\sum_{m=\varphi+1}^\infty q^m = \frac{q^{\varphi+1}}{1-q} < 2q^{\varphi+1}

Substituting this back yields:

|R_T(x)| < 2 B T^\varphi \left(\frac{\rho R}{T}\right)^{\varphi+1} = \frac{2 B (\rho R)^{\varphi+1}}{T}

Taking the limit:

\lim_{T \to +\infty} \frac{2 B (\rho R)^{\varphi+1}}{T} = 0

Therefore, on the interval |x| \le R, the remainder strictly converges to 0. Since R is arbitrary, the proposition is proven!

With this, we have established the validity of this method: as the large number increases, the net gain in y becomes a polynomial in x, successfully realizing the transformation specified above.
