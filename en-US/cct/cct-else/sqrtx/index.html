Ladder Problem

The original video of this proof (Bilibili)↗

Introduction#

The "Ladder Problem" is an important problem in the field of Coordinates Calculation Theory. Its equivalent formulation is: it is impossible to directly compute the arithmetic square root \sqrt{x} through the coordinate arithmetic of the execute command.

Part 1. All Elements Generable by execute Belong to P#

Goal: Prove that P contains all elements generable by execute.

Conventions#

P is the minimal class of partial functions in two variables (x, t), containing the following elements and closed under the following operations:

  • Initial elements: (x,t)\mapsto x; (x,t)\mapsto g(t) (for arbitrary g)
  • Three operations: c-s; \dfrac{sc}{\sqrt{s^2+b^2}} (denominator nonzero); \displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}s(x,t)

Throughout, m,n,p,q,r,a,b,c\in P always refers to elements of P.

Ⅰ.1 First Prove x^2 \in P#

Consider the element of P:

2t^2-\frac{2t^2\cdot t}{\sqrt{t^2+x^2}}\ \in\ P

As t\to+\infty, the pointwise limit exists:

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\left(2t^2-\frac{2t^3}{\sqrt{t^2+x^2}}\right)=x^2

Therefore x^2\in P.

Ⅰ.2 For Any m,n\in P, We Have mn\in P#

Clearly m+n=m-(0-n)\in P, so (m+n)^2\in P. Since

(m+n)^2=m^2+n^2+2mn

and m^2,n^2\in P, by subtraction we get 2mn\in P, and hence mn\in P. That is,

mn=\frac{(m+n)^2-m^2-n^2}{2}

P is closed under multiplication.

Ⅰ.3 For Any m,n\in P, We Have m/n\in P#

We first prove 1/n\in P. It is easy to construct

\frac{\dfrac{1}{t}\cdot t}{\sqrt{n^2+\left(\dfrac{1}{t}\right)^2}}\ \in\ P

As t\to+\infty, this proves \dfrac{1}{|n|}\in P:

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+1/t^2}}=\frac{1}{|n|}

Since \dfrac{n}{|n|}=\dfrac{n\cdot 1}{\sqrt{0^2+n^2}}\in P (a native operation), and by multiplication (Ⅰ.2):

\frac{1}{|n|}\cdot\frac{n}{|n|}=\frac{n}{n^2}=\frac{1}{n}\in P

Then by multiplication, m/n\in P. P is closed under division.

Ⅰ.4 Addition and Subtraction#

The proof for addition and subtraction of two elements is straightforward and is omitted here.

At this point we have: P is closed under all four arithmetic operations (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division), and contains x^2, the native operation \dfrac{sc}{\sqrt{s^2+b^2}}, and pointwise limits.

Ⅰ.5 Algebraic Representation and the Inductive Framework#

We employ algebraic representation and proceed by induction.

The current state of the execution chain is characterized by a set of quantities: coordinates x_{mc}, y_{mc}, z_{mc}, and the trigonometric quantities of the view angle \sin r_x, \cos r_x, \sin r_y, \cos r_y.

Inductive claim: All of these quantities, viewed as functions of x, belong to P.

Base case: x\in P. The induction starts correctly.

We now handle each possible next subcommand in turn.

Ⅰ.6 Subcommand: positioned ~a ~b ~c#

Since a,b,c\in P, the new coordinates

x_{mc}'=x_{mc}+a,\qquad y_{mc}'=y_{mc}+b,\qquad z_{mc}'=z_{mc}+c

all belong to P by closure under addition (Ⅰ.4).

The cases positioned 0 ~? ~? and other combinations are handled analogously and are omitted.

Ⅰ.7 Subcommand: positioned ^a ^b ^c#

Since the prior values \sin r_y, \sin r_x, \cos r_y, \cos r_x can each be expressed as a function of x belonging to P, by algebraic representation positioned ^a^b^c clearly also belongs to P.

Reason: The transformation from local to world coordinates is a polynomial combination (involving only addition, subtraction, and multiplication) of these trigonometric quantities and a, b, c. Since P is closed under addition, subtraction, and multiplication (Ⅰ.2, Ⅰ.4):

\text{new coordinates} = \text{old coordinates} + \text{(polynomial in }\sin r_x,\cos r_x,\sin r_y,\cos r_y\text{ and }a,b,c\text{)} \in P

Ⅰ.8 Subcommand: facing a b c#

Since the prior coordinates and a,b,c belong to P, by algebraic representation the new view angle values are given by:

\cos r_x=\frac{\sqrt{(x_{mc}-x)^2+(z_{mc}-z)^2}}{\sqrt{(x_{mc}-x)^2+(y_{mc}-y)^2+(z_{mc}-z)^2}}
\sin r_x=\frac{y_{mc}-y}{\sqrt{(x_{mc}-x)^2+(y_{mc}-y)^2+(z_{mc}-z)^2}}
\cos r_y=\frac{z_{mc}-z}{\sqrt{(x_{mc}-x)^2+(z_{mc}-z)^2}}
\sin r_y=\frac{x_{mc}-x}{\sqrt{(x_{mc}-x)^2+(z_{mc}-z)^2}}

Here \cos r_y and \sin r_y are 2D normalizations of the form \dfrac{p}{\sqrt{p^2+q^2}}, which is a native operation of P and follows directly. However, \cos r_x and \sin r_x are 3D normalizations of the form \dfrac{p}{\sqrt{p^2+q^2+r^2}}, which requires the following lemma.

Ⅰ.9 Key Lemma: 3D Normalization#

Lemma: For any p,q,r\in P,

\frac{p}{\sqrt{p^2+q^2+r^2}}\in P

Proof:

  1. The numerator p\in P is immediate.
  2. For the denominator, we rewrite by nesting:
\sqrt{p^2+q^2+r^2}=\sqrt{p^2+\left(\sqrt{q^2+r^2}\right)^2}
  1. One can verify \sqrt{q^2+r^2}\in P by taking reciprocals twice: from the native operation \dfrac{q\cdot c}{\sqrt{q^2+r^2}}\in P, combined with the reciprocal from Ⅰ.3 and multiplication from Ⅰ.2.
  2. Hence \sqrt{p^2+q^2+r^2}\in P is clear. The lemma then follows by division (Ⅰ.3). \blacksquare

It follows that \cos r_x, \sin r_x, \cos r_y, \sin r_y \in P.

Ⅰ.10 Subcommand: facing ~p ~q ~r and Combinations#

The cases facing ~ p q, facing ~p ~q ~r, and other combinations are handled analogously.

If the next subcommand is facing ^p^q^r, the new view angle trigonometric quantities are given by:

n_{\sin r_y,\text{new}}=-\frac{a\cos r_y-\big(b\sin r_x+c\cos r_x\big)\sin r_y}{\sqrt{a^2+\big(b\sin r_x+c\cos r_x\big)^2}}
n_{\cos r_y,\text{new}}=\frac{a\sin r_y+\big(b\sin r_x+c\cos r_x\big)\cos r_y}{\sqrt{a^2+\big(b\sin r_x+c\cos r_x\big)^2}}
n_{\sin r_x,\text{new}}=\frac{c\sin r_x-b\cos r_x}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}
n_{\cos r_x,\text{new}}=\frac{\sqrt{a^2+\big(b\sin r_x+c\cos r_x\big)^2}}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}

The numerators of all four expressions are polynomials in known elements of P; the denominators are of the 2D form \sqrt{a^2+(\cdot)^2} (a native operation) and the 3D form \sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2} (Lemma Ⅰ.9). By closure under division (Ⅰ.3), the new \sin r_x, \cos r_x, \sin r_y, \cos r_y \in P follows.

Cases facing ~ p q, facing ~p ~q ~r, and other combinations are handled analogously.

Ⅰ.11 Subcommand: rotated ~a ~b#

With a,b\in P, this follows immediately from the angle addition formulas for trigonometric functions:

\sin(r+a)=\sin r\cos a+\cos r\sin a
\cos(r+a)=\cos r\cos a-\sin r\sin a

The right-hand sides contain only the old trigonometric quantities and \sin a, \cos a (which are functions of x independent of t, hence in P) combined by addition, subtraction, and multiplication, and are closed by Ⅰ.2 and Ⅰ.4.

Conclusion#

After executing each subcommand, the coordinates and the four trigonometric quantities all remain in P, completing the induction.

\boxed{\text{The set }P\text{ contains all elements generable by \texttt{execute}.}}

Part 2. \sqrt{x} \notin P#

0. Conventions and Goal#

Elements of P_n are partial functions on (x,t)\in[0,\infty)^2: x denotes the projection (x,t)\mapsto x (totally defined), and g(t) denotes any g:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R (constant in x, totally defined). For f\in P_n and fixed t, write the section

f_t(x)=f(x,t),\qquad \operatorname{dom}f_t\subseteq[0,\infty).

The domain of a composed term follows the pointwise convention: \operatorname{dom}(c-s)_t=\operatorname{dom}c_t\cap\operatorname{dom}s_t;
\operatorname{dom}\Big(\tfrac{sc}{\sqrt{s^2+b^2}}\Big)_t=\operatorname{dom}s_t\cap\operatorname{dom}b_t\cap\operatorname{dom}c_t

(the denominator is required to be pointwise nonzero). For the limit term h(x)=\lim_{t\to+\infty}s(x,t),
\operatorname{dom}h=\{x:\exists T(x)<\infty,\ s(x,t)\ \text{is defined for}\ t\ge T(x)\ \text{and converges as}\ t\to+\infty\},

h is constant in t, so \operatorname{dom}h_t=\operatorname{dom}h.

Goal: the function \sigma:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R, \sigma(x)=\sqrt x, does not belong to P.

1. The Invariant: Totally-Real Witness Polynomials#

Definition 1. Let \mathcal T\subseteq\mathbb R[x,y]\setminus\{0\} be the set of polynomials satisfying the following condition: for every x_0\in\mathbb R, if A(x_0,y)\not\equiv0, then all roots of A(x_0,y)\in\mathbb C[y] are real.

Definition 2. Let D\subseteq\mathbb R and f:D\to\mathbb R. Say A\in\mathcal T is a witness for f if A(x,f(x))=0 for all x\in D. Say f is d-TR if there exists a witness of degree \le d.

Two trivial but useful facts:

  • (R1) Restriction closure: A is a witness for f on D \Rightarrow for any D'\subseteq D, A is a witness for f|_{D'}. (This is the technical foundation that makes the patching in Step 2 work.)
  • (R2) Scaling closure: \lambda\ne0\Rightarrow\lambda A\in\mathcal T, and it is still a witness.

Remark. y^2-x\notin\mathcal T: at x_0=-1 we get y^2+1, whose roots are \pm i.

2. Lemma 1 (Continuous Dependence of Roots) and Corollaries#

Lemma 1. Let p_k, p\in\mathbb C[y] have degrees uniformly \le d, with coefficient vectors p_k\to p, and \deg p=m\ge1. Then every root of p is the limit of some sequence of roots of p_k.

Proof. Let p(\alpha)=0. Choose \varepsilon>0 so that p has no zeros on \partial B(\alpha,\varepsilon), and let \delta=\min_{\partial B}|p|>0. Convergence of coefficients implies p_k\to p uniformly on the compact set \overline{B}, so for large k, |p_k-p|<\delta\le|p| on \partial B. By Rouché's theorem, p_k has at least as many zeros in B(\alpha,\varepsilon) as p, hence \ge1. Let \varepsilon\downarrow0 and take a diagonal subsequence. \square

Corollary 1a (Membership in \mathcal T via a Dense Set). Let A\in\mathbb R[x,y]\setminus\{0\}, E\subseteq\mathbb R dense (e.g., cofinite), and suppose for each x_0\in E that A(x_0,\cdot)\not\equiv0 and all its roots are real. Then A\in\mathcal T.

Proof. Let x_0 be arbitrary with A(x_0,\cdot)\not\equiv0. If \deg A(x_0,\cdot)=0 there are no roots and the conclusion is trivial. Otherwise take E\ni x_j\to x_0; since the coefficients are polynomials in x, we have A(x_j,\cdot)\to A(x_0,\cdot) with degrees uniformly bounded. By Lemma 1, every root of A(x_0,\cdot) is the limit of a sequence of real numbers, hence real. \square

Corollary 1b (\mathcal T is closed under coefficient limits). Let A_k\in\mathcal T with \deg A_k\le d, and suppose the coefficient vectors converge to A^*\ne0. Then A^*\in\mathcal T.

Proof. Take x_0 such that A^*(x_0,\cdot)\not\equiv0. If infinitely many k satisfy A_k(x_0,\cdot)\equiv0, passing to the limit along that subsequence gives A^*(x_0,\cdot)\equiv0, a contradiction; so for almost all k, A_k(x_0,\cdot)\not\equiv0 with all real roots. By Lemma 1 (when \deg A^*(x_0,\cdot)\ge1) the conclusion follows. \square

3. Lemma 2 (The Three Algebraic Operations Preserve TR)#

Lemma 2. Let D\subseteq\mathbb R be infinite, and let f,g:D\to\mathbb R have witnesses P(x,z), Q(x,w) of degrees \le d respectively. Then

f-g,\qquad fg,\qquad \sqrt{f^2+g^2}

on D, and (when g is pointwise nonzero on D) f/g on D, are all C(d)-TR, where C(d) depends only on d.

Proof. Write the relation polynomial for each of the four cases:

N(x,y,z,w)=\;y-z+w,\quad y-zw,\quad y^2-z^2-w^2,\quad yw-z .

Step 0 (Preprocessing and degree lower bound). First prove \deg_z P\ge1: otherwise P=p(x), and p(x)=0 on the infinite set D forces p\equiv0, contradicting P\ne0. Likewise \deg_w Q\ge1.

For the quotient case, do an extra preprocessing step (this is the key to patching the ambiguity "g\equiv0" in the original proof): let k\ge0 be maximal such that w^k\mid Q, and set \tilde Q=Q/w^k. Then:

  • \tilde Q(x,g(x))=0 on D (since g(x)\ne0);
  • \tilde Q\in\mathcal T: for each x_0, the root set of \tilde Q(x_0,\cdot) is contained in that of Q(x_0,\cdot) (when \tilde Q(x_0,\cdot)\not\equiv0, we have Q(x_0,\cdot)=w^k\tilde Q(x_0,\cdot)\not\equiv0);
  • \tilde Q(x,0)\not\equiv0 (otherwise w\mid\tilde Q).

For the other three cases set \tilde Q=Q. Write q=\deg_w\tilde Q\ge1 (by the same argument as above).

Step 1 (Construction). Set

B(x,y,z)=\operatorname{Res}_w\big(\tilde Q(x,w),\,N(x,y,z,w)\big),\qquad A(x,y)=\operatorname{Res}_z\big(P(x,z),\,B(x,y,z)\big),

where the resultants are defined via the Sylvester matrix with respect to \deg_w\tilde Q=q, \deg_w N=e, \deg_z P, and \deg_z B. By the degree estimate for the Sylvester determinant, \deg A\le C(d), where C depends only on d.

Step 2 (A vanishes at the correct points). The resultant belongs to the elimination ideal: there exist \gamma,\delta such that B=\gamma\tilde Q+\delta N, and \alpha,\beta such that A=\alpha P+\beta B. Take x\in D, z=f(x), w=g(x), and y=\varphi(f(x),g(x)) (taking the positive root in the square root case). Then \tilde Q(x,w)=0 and N(x,y,z,w)=0, so B(x,y,z)=0; and P(x,z)=0, so

A\big(x,\varphi(f(x),g(x))\big)=0\qquad(x\in D).

Step 3 (Good point set E). Let

E=\{x_0\in\mathbb R:\ \operatorname{lc}_zP(x_0)\ne0,\ \operatorname{lc}_w\tilde Q(x_0)\ne0,\ \tilde Q(x_0,0)\ne0\ (\text{quotient case only})\}.

This is a cofinite set. For x_0\in E, since both leading coefficients are nonzero, the specialization of the Sylvester determinant is faithful: if the actual degree drops upon specialization, then
A(x_0,y_0)=\operatorname{lc}_zP(x_0)^{\,\ast}\cdot\operatorname{Res}_z\big(P(x_0,\cdot),B(x_0,y_0,\cdot)\big)

(the inner one similarly carries a nonzero power of \operatorname{lc}_w\tilde Q(x_0)), so whether A(x_0,y_0)=0 is consistent with the specialized resultant. In what follows we use only this zero/nonzero distinction.

Step 4 (Roots are all real). Fix x_0\in E. Let z_1,\dots,z_p be the roots of P(x_0,\cdot) (p\ge1, all real), and w_1,\dots,w_q the roots of \tilde Q(x_0,\cdot) (q\ge1, all real, and all nonzero in the quotient case). Suppose y_0\in\mathbb C satisfies A(x_0,y_0)=0.

(4a) Extracting z_i. By Step 3, \operatorname{Res}_z\big(P(x_0,\cdot),B(x_0,y_0,\cdot)\big)=0, with \operatorname{lc}_z P(x_0)\ne0 and \deg_z P(x_0,\cdot)=p\ge1.

  • If \deg_z B(x_0,y_0,\cdot)\ge1: the resultant is zero \Rightarrow they share a common root, i.e., there exists i with B(x_0,y_0,z_i)=0.
  • If B(x_0,y_0,\cdot) is a nonzero constant c: the resultant =c^{\,p}\ne0, contradicting the assumption; this case does not occur.
  • If B(x_0,y_0,\cdot)\equiv0: then B(x_0,y_0,z_i)=0 for every i; take i=1.

In all cases there exists a real number z:=z_i with B(x_0,y_0,z)=0.

(4b) Extracting w_j. That is, \operatorname{Res}_w\big(\tilde Q(x_0,\cdot),N(x_0,y_0,z,\cdot)\big)=0, with \operatorname{lc}_w\tilde Q(x_0)\ne0 and q\ge1. Consider \deg_w N(x_0,y_0,z,\cdot):

  • Difference: N=y_0-z+w, degree 1;
  • Square root: N=y_0^2-z^2-w^2, degree 2;
  • Product: N=y_0-zw; if z\ne0, degree 1; if z=0, then N\equiv y_0 (constant), so the resultant =y_0^{\,q}=0\Rightarrow y_0=0\in\mathbb R, done;
  • Quotient: N=y_0w-z; if y_0\ne0, degree 1; if y_0=0, it is already real, done.

So in the unfinished cases \deg_w N\ge1, and the zero resultant yields a common root: there exists j with N(x_0,y_0,z,w_j)=0, and z, w_j\in\mathbb R.

(4c) Conclusion. Case analysis:

  • Difference: y_0=z-w_j\in\mathbb R;
  • Product: y_0=zw_j\in\mathbb R;
  • Quotient: y_0w_j=z, and by the preprocessing w_j\ne0, so y_0=z/w_j\in\mathbb R;
  • Square root: y_0^2=z^2+w_j^2\ge0, so y_0=\pm\sqrt{z^2+w_j^2}\in\mathbb R.

This step is the sole place where the restriction "the denominator must be the square root of a sum of squares" takes effect: the radicand is nonnegative at every real point and every conjugate.

Step 5 (A\not\equiv0). Still fixing x_0\in E: Step 4 shows that every root y_0 of A(x_0,\cdot) must satisfy some equation N(x_0,y_0,z_i,w_j)=0 (or fall into the two explicit cases y_0=0 in (4b)). For each pair (i,j), the polynomial N(x_0,\cdot,z_i,w_j) in y is not identically zero: the difference and product cases give y-\text{const}; the quotient case gives w_jy-z_i with w_j\ne0; the square root case gives y^2-(z_i^2+w_j^2). Hence the roots of A(x_0,\cdot) lie in a finite set of at most 2pq+1 elements, so A(x_0,\cdot)\not\equiv0, and thus A\not\equiv0.

Step 6. By Steps 4 and 5 together with Corollary 1a (applied to the dense cofinite set E), we get A\in\mathcal T; by Step 2, A is a witness for \varphi(f,g) on D. \square

4. Lemma 3 (Pointwise Limits Preserve TR)#

Lemma 3. Let s be a partial function of (x,t), let h(x)=\lim_{t\to+\infty}s(x,t), and suppose \operatorname{dom}h is uncountable. Suppose there exist d and n_0 such that for each t\ge n_0, the section s_t has a witness A_t of degree \le d on its entire domain \operatorname{dom}s_t. Then h is d-TR on all of \operatorname{dom}h.

Proof. By (R2) we may normalize \|A_t\|=1 (Euclidean norm of the coefficient vector). Bivariate real polynomials of degree \le d form a finite-dimensional space whose unit sphere is compact, so there exists t_k\to+\infty (t_k\ge n_0) with A_{t_k}\to A^*, \|A^*\|=1, hence A^*\ne0; by Corollary 1b, A^*\in\mathcal T.

Take any x\in\operatorname{dom}h. By definition there exists T(x)<\infty such that for t\ge T(x), s(x,t) is defined and s(x,t)\to h(x). Choose K such that k\ge K\Rightarrow t_k\ge T(x); then x\in\operatorname{dom}s_{t_k}, so A_{t_k}(x,s(x,t_k))=0. Since polynomials are continuous in their coefficients and arguments, letting k\to\infty gives

A^*(x,h(x))=0.

Note this holds at every point of \operatorname{dom}h, not merely on some subset. \square

5. The Inductive Proposition#

Proposition. For each n and each f\in P_n, there exists d(f)<\infty such that

(\star)\qquad \text{for every }t\ge0,\ \text{if }\operatorname{dom}f_t\text{ is uncountable, then }f_t\text{ has a witness of degree}\le d(f)\text{ on }\operatorname{dom}f_t.

Proof (by induction on n).

n=0. f=x: take y-x; for every x_0 the unique root is x_0\in\mathbb R, so it belongs to \mathcal T, with d=1. f=g(t): for fixed t take y-g(t)\in\mathcal T, with d=1.

Difference and quotient compositions. Suppose f=c-s with c,s\in P_n, and let t be such that D:=\operatorname{dom}f_t=\operatorname{dom}c_t\cap\operatorname{dom}s_t is uncountable. Then \operatorname{dom}c_t, \operatorname{dom}s_t\supseteq D are also uncountable, so by the inductive hypothesis each has a witness; by (R1), restricting to D yields witnesses for c_t and s_t on D. Since D is uncountable it is in particular infinite, so Lemma 2 gives a witness for f_t on D of degree \le C(\max(d(c),d(s))), a bound independent of t.

Suppose f=\dfrac{s\,c}{\sqrt{s^2+b^2}} with t such that D:=\operatorname{dom}f_t is uncountable. Then \operatorname{dom}s_t, \operatorname{dom}b_t, \operatorname{dom}c_t\supseteq D are uncountable, and the inductive hypothesis together with (R1) give witnesses for s_t, b_t, c_t on D. Apply Lemma 2 in three steps: u=\sqrt{s_t^2+b_t^2} is TR on D; v=s_tc_t is TR on D; since u is pointwise nonzero on D by assumption, f_t=v/u is TR on D. The degree bound is a three-fold iteration of C, depending only on d(s), d(b), d(c), and independent of t.

(This is patching point 1: by formulating (\star) as a conditional "if the domain is uncountable, then …", the domain of a subterm contains the domain of the composed term, so the uncountability hypothesis propagates downward automatically, with no additional assumptions needed.)

Limit. Suppose f=h where h(x)=\lim_{t\to+\infty}s(x,t) with s\in P_n, and \operatorname{dom}h is uncountable (h is constant in t, so all sections are h itself). Set

E_n=\{x\in\operatorname{dom}h:\ T(x)\le n\}\quad(n\in\mathbb N),\qquad \operatorname{dom}h=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb N}E_n.

A countable union must have some uncountable member: choose n_0 such that E_{n_0} is uncountable. For every t\ge n_0 we have \operatorname{dom}s_t\supseteq E_{n_0}, so \operatorname{dom}s_t is uncountable, and by the inductive hypothesis s_t has a witness of degree \le d(s) on its entire domain. Lemma 3 then applies, and h is d(s)-TR on \operatorname{dom}h. \square

(This is patching point 2: E_{n_0} is used solely to guarantee that for all sufficiently large t, \operatorname{dom}s_t is large enough to invoke the inductive hypothesis. Once we have A_{t_k} holding on the entire \operatorname{dom}s_{t_k}, the pointwise argument of Lemma 3 covers every point of \operatorname{dom}h, so the conclusion remains in the form of (\star), and the induction closes. This also explains why (\star) must require the witness to hold on the entire sectional domain, and cannot permit a countable set of exceptions.)

6. Conclusion#

Suppose for contradiction that \sigma\in P, i.e., the function \sigma:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R, \sigma(x)=\sqrt x, belongs to some P_n. It is constant in t, so every section is \sigma itself, with domain [0,\infty) uncountable. By the Proposition, there exists A^*\in\mathcal T such that

A^*(x,\sqrt x)=0\qquad(\forall x\ge0).

(a) (y^2-x)\mid A^*. Substitute x=u^2: A^*(u^2,u)=0 for all u\ge0, which is a univariate polynomial vanishing on an infinite set, so A^*(u^2,u)\equiv0 in \mathbb R[u]. The ring isomorphism \mathbb R[x,y]/(y^2-x)\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\mathbb R[y] (sending x\mapsto y^2, y\mapsto y) maps A^* to A^*(y^2,y)=0, so A^*\in(y^2-x).

(b) Stripping factors of (x+1). Let m\ge0 be maximal such that (x+1)^m\mid A^*, and set R=A^*/(x+1)^m, so that R(-1,y)\not\equiv0. For x_0\ne-1, since A^*(x_0,\cdot)=(x_0+1)^mR(x_0,\cdot) differs from R(x_0,\cdot) by a nonzero constant, the roots of R(x_0,\cdot) are all real whenever R(x_0,\cdot)\not\equiv0. By Corollary 1a (applied to the dense cofinite set E=\mathbb R\setminus\{-1\}, after excluding the finitely many x_0 for which R(x_0,\cdot)\equiv0 — there can only be finitely many, otherwise R\equiv0) we get R\in\mathcal T. Since y^2-x is irreducible in \mathbb R[x,y] (quadratic in y, with no root in \mathbb R[x], as x is not a perfect square) and is not associate to x+1, it follows that (y^2-x)\mid R.

(c) Contradiction. Write R=(y^2-x)S. Then R(-1,y)=(y^2+1)S(-1,y). Since R(-1,y)\not\equiv0, we have S(-1,y)\not\equiv0, so R(-1,\cdot) is a nonzero polynomial divisible by y^2+1, hence it has the non-real roots \pm i. This contradicts R\in\mathcal T.

Therefore \sqrt x\notin P. \blacksquare
